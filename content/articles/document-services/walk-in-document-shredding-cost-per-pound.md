You have a box of papers that needs to disappear — tax returns past the retention window, bank statements, medical bills — and every search for a price ends the same way: a form asking for your name, email, and phone number so a sales rep can call you back with a quote. The right outcome is simple: a price you can see before you commit, a shredding that happens the same day, and proof it happened.

Three ways to pay for the same box — per pound at a counter, per box from a truck, per quote from a national vendor. Only one of them lets you watch. Here is what each one actually costs.

## The Quote-Only Pricing Model

The problem has a name: the quote-only pricing model.

Mobile shredding trucks are real businesses built for a real customer — the office manager clearing out a records room. The economics only work above a certain volume: the truck costs the same to dispatch whether you have two boxes or two hundred, so the price is set to make the two-hundred-box job profitable and the two-box job irrelevant. Look at how the biggest name in the industry describes its own pricing. Shred-it's pricing page states that for one-time services, "pricing is based on the volume and container type (box size or quantity)" and that "minimum fees apply and vary by location." Its current bulk-discount terms go further: 25% off for orders of $1,000 or more, 10% off for orders under $1,000, and a "minimum order value of $250 (before tax)" just to qualify for the smaller discount. Read those numbers for what they tell you about the customer the pricing was designed for.

None of this is a scam. For a business purging a decade of files, a truck that comes to the loading dock is worth every penny. But if you are a homeowner with one box of old statements, or a sole proprietor closing out a client file, that pricing model has no slot for you. The minimum fee for a job that would weigh ten pounds at a retail counter is a floor built for someone else's job. And because the price arrives as a phone quote, you have no way to comparison-shop it — you are trusting one dispatcher's number for your ZIP code.

## Waiting on a Callback That Sets the Price

You fill out the quote form anyway, because you have nothing else. A representative calls back the next day. The number is per box, not per pound, and it comes with a service window: the truck can be at your house Tuesday, sometime between 8 AM and 5 PM. You take a day off work. The driver arrives, feeds your boxes into the truck's shredder while you watch through the cab's camera monitor — that part is genuinely good — and hands you a certificate. Then you do the math on the drive home. You paid a truck-dispatch price to destroy a pile that would have cost pocket change at a counter.

Or you skip the truck and take the box to a retail chain. At a FedEx Office that offers shredding, a team member weighs your documents and the price is $1.49 per pound — but per FedEx's own page, the service is available "at select locations" only, and the papers go into "locked shred bins" for transport to a certified shredding provider. You get a fair per-pound rate and an off-site handoff you cannot watch. The price is visible; the destruction is not.

Both routes cost you something the price tag does not show: one costs scheduling, the other costs custody.

## One Machine, One Scale — That Is the Whole Product

You rearranged your week so a stranger could watch your bank statements go into a truck. Or you paid by the pound and never saw the machine that ate them. ## What the Box Costs You to Keep

The stack does not price itself. **Time:** every week a quote form sits unanswered, the box stays on the shelf and the errand stays on the list. **Money:** drive a box to a quote-only provider or take a day off for a truck window and you are paying scheduling costs the per-pound rate never charges. **Security:** the honest risk is physical — a box of intact account numbers on a garage shelf, in a car trunk during a soccer game, or curbside on trash day, is worth more to a thief than anything on your laptop. Paper does not breach; it leaks, one recycling bin at a time. And every time you walk past that shelf you do the same math and put it off again.
