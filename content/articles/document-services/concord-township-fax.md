## The Fax Trap

You're thinking: why does the title company still demand a fax in 2026? Because their system never got the memo that the machines vanished — and now you're the one left holding the page. That's the whole trap: a mandatory technology with a disappearing supply.

On one side, entire industries still run on fax. Title companies, courts, lenders, medical offices, and government agencies treat faxed documents as more reliable or more secure than email, and many won't accept anything else. Ask them why and you'll hear about compliance, chain of custody, or "that's just how our system works." The requirement is not going away on any schedule you can wait for.

On the other side, the machines have vanished. Home office catalogs dropped fax hardware a decade ago. Libraries and big-box office stores have quietly retired their public fax stations or left them broken. The offices that still demand faxes assume someone, somewhere, still has a machine — and that someone is you, on a Tuesday, with a deadline.

That's the trap: a mandatory technology with a disappearing supply. That sticky note with the fax number in your hand? It doesn't make you a relic. It makes you the unlucky bridge between a 1995 rule and a 2026 world that threw the bridge away — and the gap lands on whoever needs to send one page, today.

## What It Actually Feels Like

It starts with a phone call. "Can you get that over to us by five? Fax is fine." You say yes, because you have the document and, you figure, how hard can it be?

Then the hunt begins. You check the drawers at home — no machine, obviously. You remember the library near you had a public fax. You call. It's been out of service since spring. The office supply store on the strip near Mentor says their machine is self-service, near the back, and there may be a line. You drive eleven minutes to get there, park, walk past the registers, and find the machine with a taped-on sign: out of paper, or out of order, or "ask an associate" — and the associate is with three other customers.

Back at home you search for online fax services, and now you're creating an account, entering a card number, uploading a scan of a document you were handed on paper — for one page, once. And the worst part isn't the driving or the fees. It's the uncertainty. When the machine finally chirps, did the other end actually get it? A fax sent from a stranger's jammed self-service machine comes with no proof, no receipt, no record. If the title company says it never arrived, you have nothing to show.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Sending a document to a number and confirming it arrived is one of the oldest solved problems in office technology. It should not require owning hardware that manufacturers stopped making, or a scavenger hunt across Lake County, or a subscription for a one-time errand.

The failure is structural, not personal. The offices that require fax built their intake around a machine that other parts of the economy were busy deleting. Nobody coordinated. So the duty to bridge the gap — the hunt, the driving, the failed machines, the wondering whether it went through — got pushed onto you, at whatever moment the deadline lands. A one-page transmission shouldn't cost you an afternoon, and it certainly shouldn't leave you hoping the other end got it. Knowing it went through should be part of the transaction, not a luxury.
