## The WebCheck Maze

The state of Ohio uses an electronic fingerprinting system called WebCheck. It is managed by the Ohio Attorney General's Bureau of Criminal Investigation (BCI). When an employer, licensing board, or adoption agency requires a background check, they will tell you which type you need. But they rarely tell you where to get it done.

Here are the three most common types of background checks in Ohio:

**BCI Background Check (Ohio Only).** This checks your criminal history against the Ohio Records Database maintained by BCI. It covers arrests, convictions, and ongoing cases within the state of Ohio. If you have lived in Ohio your whole life and are applying for a state license or a local job, this is usually what you need.

**BCI & FBI Background Check.** This runs your prints through both the Ohio database and the national FBI database. It checks for criminal history across all fifty states. This is required for positions involving children, healthcare, and certain professional licenses. Teachers, nurses, daycare workers, foster parents, and adoption applicants almost always need this level of check.

**ODE Background Check (Ohio Department of Education).** This is a specific variant for educators and school employees. It combines BCI and FBI checks with additional education-specific screening. If you are applying for a teaching license or a non-teaching position in a school district, this is the one.

Each type requires the same fingerprinting process — the difference is what the prints get checked against on the back end. And the process itself is simple, but finding a reliable fingerprinting location often is not.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You show up at the sheriff's office in Painesville at 9:00 AM for your appointment — the only slot they had available this week. You fill out a paper form. You wait in a lobby with three other people. The deputy calls you back, rolls your fingerprints on an ink card — old-school, messy, easy to smudge. They scan the card into the digital system. You pay the fee. You leave.

Three days later, you get a call: "Your prints were rejected. The ink card was too light. You need to come back and do it again."

You schedule another appointment. You drive back to Painesville. You fill out the same form. You wait again. The deputy does another card. This one goes through.

Total time invested: two trips, two appointments, about three hours of driving and waiting, plus the delay in your background check processing.

The rejection rate for ink-and-card fingerprinting is significant. Smudged prints, light impressions, incomplete rolls — all of these cause the electronic scan to fail. And when it fails, you start over.

Digital fingerprinting solves this. The scanner captures your prints electronically on the first try, with real-time feedback so the technician can see immediately if the quality is acceptable. There is no card to smudge, no ink to dry, no second trip required.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Fingerprinting is a straightforward process. You press your fingers on a scanner. It takes about ten minutes. The technology has existed for decades. There is no reason a simple identity verification step should require two appointments, an hour of driving, and a week of waiting to discover whether your prints were good enough.

But the legacy system — ink cards processed through county sheriff's offices — persists because it was the only option for a long time. Many sheriff's offices and police departments still use it, even though digital fingerprinting is faster, more accurate, and less prone to rejection.

The philosophical problem is this: a background check should not be bottlenecked by the printing method. You should be able to walk into a location, get scanned digitally, and walk out knowing your prints went through — not hoping the ink card did not get smudged.

## Who Needs Fingerprinting in Ohio

If you are wondering whether you need to be fingerprinted, here are the most common scenarios:

- **Educators and school employees.** Teachers, substitutes, bus drivers, cafeteria workers, aides, and volunteers in Ohio schools all require BCI & FBI background checks.
- **Healthcare professionals.** Nurses, doctors, pharmacists, nursing home staff, home health aides, and anyone working with vulnerable adults.
- **Childcare workers.** Daycare centers, preschools, and in-home childcare providers.
- **Foster parents and adoptive parents.** The Ohio Department of Job and Family Services requires fingerprint-based background checks for all foster and adoptive parents.
- **Real estate professionals.** Real estate license applicants in Ohio must undergo a BCI background check.
- **Professional license applicants.** Many licensing boards — including cosmetology, accounting, counseling, social work, and securities — require fingerprint-based checks.
- **Volunteers.** Schools, youth sports leagues, and nonprofit organizations increasingly require background checks for volunteers who work with children.

If you are in any of these categories and have been told you need a background check, you need fingerprinting. And you want it done right the first time.
