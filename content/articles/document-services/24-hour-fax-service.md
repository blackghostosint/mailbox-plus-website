## What Should Take Two Minutes

Your deadline is 5 p.m. Your workday ends at 5 p.m. And the document — a signed agreement, an insurance claim, a records request — can only travel by fax, because the office on the other end says so. That is the whole bind, and you already know it: the page is ready, the number is written down, and there is no machine between you and the deadline.

It should work like any other errand: hand over a page, dial, walk out with proof it went through. At Mailbox Plus in Concord Township that is exactly what it takes — and when the deadline lands after our doors close, there is a drop-off that puts your fax first in line the next business morning.

## Say It Plainly: The Nine-to-Five Fax Trap

Here is the mechanism nobody planned. Fax never died where institutions need it — title agencies, courts, medical offices, lenders — because a fax is a point-to-point transmission over the phone network with a transmission record at the end. What died is the infrastructure around it: home machines went to the curb, libraries unplugged theirs, and the self-service terminals left inside big-box stores are outsourced kiosks that a store clerk cannot fix when the payment screen times out.

Now stack the hours on top. The institutions that require fax operate 9 to 5. The retail infrastructure that could send one operates 9 to 5 too — same window, same lunch hours, same Saturday cut-off at 2. So the demand and the supply are both awake during exactly the same eight hours that you are at work. A "24-hour fax service" search is really a search for an exit from that trap, and the market has mostly not built one.

## The Part That Wears You Down

It starts on your lunch break, because that is the only slot you have. Twenty minutes to the library — machine down, or removed last year. The office-supply kiosk scans your card, spins, and times out. You try again. A page jams halfway. The line behind you grows. And somewhere in the middle of all that, the clock on the wall keeps doing the one thing you cannot allow it to do: run toward 5 p.m.

You are not disorganized and you are not behind the times. You got caught between an institutional requirement that will not retire and a retail world that already did — and the only hours both of them keep are the hours you do not have.

## Simple Things, Made Complicated on Purpose

A fax is one page and one phone number. The technology has not changed in thirty years. There is no honest reason the transaction should cost you a lunch hour, a failed kiosk, and a guess about whether the other end received it.

And yet look at what the market offers the after-hours sender instead: consumer fax apps that want a subscription and a card number before they will even show you the send screen, plus a printer-scanner you probably do not own. Or "open 24 hours" businesses whose fax counter is a locked kiosk with an out-of-order sign. The complication is not the fax. The complication is that everyone sold you a workaround instead of the two-minute errand.
