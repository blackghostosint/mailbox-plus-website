## The Box You Keep Meaning to Deal With

You have a box of papers you do not want anyone to find — old tax returns, bank statements, medical bills, maybe ten years of a side business's records printed from your kitchen table, every customer's name and address staring up at you. It is too sensitive for the recycling bin, and you know it. The right outcome is simple: the papers go from your hands to destroyed, in one trip, with proof it actually happened.

Instead, the big retail chains turn that simple trip into a handoff you cannot see. Your documents get weighed, dropped into a locked console, and then — hours or weeks later — a third-party truck hauls them somewhere else to be destroyed. Here is how walk-in shredding works at Mailbox Plus in Concord Township: we weigh your documents at the counter, shred them on-site immediately, and hand you a signed Certificate of Destruction before you leave. Here is exactly how the two paths differ, and why the difference matters more than a few cents a pound.

## The Locked-Console Handoff

The problem has a name: the off-site handoff.

It works like this, and it is completely legal and completely normal in the retail shredding industry. FedEx Office states on its own shredding page that documents placed in locked shred bins are "securely transported off-site for destruction by a certified shredding provider." The UPS Store says the same thing differently: its shredding is provided by Iron Mountain, a national document destruction vendor, and your papers go into "the secured, locked shredding bin" when you visit. FedEx Office offers shredding at select locations only, currently at $1.49 per pound.

None of this is a scam, and none of it is a violation. It is the economics of scale: a big chain cannot put an industrial shredder in every store, so the bin is the product and the truck is the pipeline. But look at what the bin actually buys you from a chain-of-custody standpoint. Your documents — the ones with your Social Security number, your account numbers, your signature — sit intact in a locked box that other people can and do carry. Someone else transports them. Someone else destroys them, at a facility you will never see, on a schedule you were never told. The paper trail ends the moment the lid closes.

A locked bin is a claim, not a fact. You did not watch anything get destroyed. You have no way to know what happened between the counter and the truck. For most junk mail, that is fine. For your tax returns and medical records, "most likely fine" is a strange thing to accept as a security standard.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You carry two banker's boxes in from the car, one stacked on the other because the handles cut in. The clerk slides the first box across the counter and weighs it. Then your documents disappear into a slot in a gray console bolted to the floor — and that is the last time you ever see them.

You get a receipt for the weight. Not for the destruction. You pull into the driveway, engine ticking as it cools, and glance at the empty passenger seat where the box sat. For the first week or two it stays with you: that gray console bolted to the store floor, its metal lid rattling on a truck somewhere, your Social Security number riding shotgun with a stranger you will never meet. The system asked you to trade certainty for convenience and then stopped answering questions.

Compare that to the home shredder, the other dead end. Ten sheets in and the motor smells hot. Staples jam it. It spits paper dust on the carpet. Two hours of Saturday later, you have shredded one folder and reboxed the rest. Neither option respects what those papers actually are: your entire financial identity, still intact.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Destroying a document is a two-minute job. There is no chemical process, no scheduling constraint, no regulation that requires your papers to leave your sight before they are destroyed. The only reason the handoff exists is that a store without its own shredder has to buy destruction from someone who has one.

A person's financial and medical history should not travel in a locked box on someone else's truck to be destroyed in a building they will never stand in. Destruction should happen where the customer can verify it — the same standard the security industry already applies everywhere else. When a document is destroyed in front of you, there is no gap. No gap means no breach to worry about, no vendor to trust, no "the truck comes Thursdays" to memorize. It is the difference between hearing that your money is safe and watching the vault door close.
