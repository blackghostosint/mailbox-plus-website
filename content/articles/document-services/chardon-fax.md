A document has to go by fax. You don't own a machine, the library's is broken, and the form won't move any other way.

## The Fax Trap, and Why It Keeps Happening

Your tech skills aren't the problem. The problem is a requirement, written into how entire industries operate, that some documents move by fax and nothing else.

Lenders, insurers, and some county offices still run on fax lines — because their compliance trail depends on it, not because it works. The fax didn't survive because it's good. It survived because somebody's rulebook depends on it — and that somebody isn't you.

So the requirement lands on your desk with no machine attached. Your home office has a computer, a printer, maybe a scanner — and none of it satisfies the rule. The library's single fax is busy, broken, or inconvenient when you're already stretched thin. The trap isn't your failure. It's a system shipping a 1980s obligation to people who live in 2026.

## The Weight of a Hundred Small Errands

The document sits on your desk. You glance at it, then at the clock, doing the math: fifteen minutes down Route 44, find the place, wait, drive back.

And there's a second layer under the logistics. Will the machine eat the page? Did it actually go through? Will anyone on the other end confirm it landed? A fax isn't junk — it's a closing page, a medical record, a signed form. The longer it sits, the heavier it gets.

## This Wasn't Supposed To Be This Hard

The county still won't accept email for certain filings. That's the rule. The lender wants the faxed original. That's theirs. None of these rules were built for your convenience — they were built for the institutions' paperwork trails, and you're the one paying the friction.

You live in a town that runs on both maple festivals and fiber internet. You shouldn't need a thermal paper machine in your closet to participate in either.
