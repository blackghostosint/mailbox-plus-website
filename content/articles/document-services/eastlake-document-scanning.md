## The Paper Avalanche

The box isn't the problem. The problem is the system that built the box, and it's worth naming: call it **The Paper Avalanche**.

Every institution you deal with — the hospital, the county auditor, the bank, the tax agencies — still mails you one physical copy of the most important documents of your life, on paper, once. Then it's on you. If you file it, you have to be able to find it years later. If you don't, it joins the pile. No one hands you a digital copy of your deed at closing "just in case." Nobody emails you a backup of your 2015 tax return. The paper copy is the only copy, and the only filing cabinet assigned to protect it is the one you bought at an office supply store in 2011.

That's the mechanism, and it runs in plain sight. Paper accumulates because every organization pushes the storage burden onto you. It takes physical space because there's no automatic off-ramp. And it becomes unfindable because human memory — "I think that was in the green folder?" — is a terrible indexing system. You're not disorganized. You were handed a decade of single-copy paper records and a shoebox. Nobody could win that game.

## What It Actually Feels Like

When the county auditor's office asks for your settlement statement, you're already on your knees on the basement floor, flashlight in hand, elbow-deep in the box. Your fingertips find receipts, warranties for appliances you no longer own, a manila envelope that says "IMPORTANT" with nothing written on the outside saying what's important.

You pull folders out and put them back. You check the clock — 7:40, and you still haven't started dinner. Your back hurts from bending over a plastic bin that was never designed for this. Somewhere in there is the paper you need, or it isn't, and either way you're going to stop looking in twenty minutes and feel the specific guilt of a job abandoned halfway. That's the tax the Paper Avalanche charges: not just the space in your closet, but twenty minutes of your evening, every single time you need proof of your own life.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Owning your records should mean you can pull them up, not dig for them. If the county can look up your parcel in seconds, you should be able to look up your own closing documents in seconds too.

And there's a stakes question nobody likes to think about: paper burns. Paper floods. Basements in Lake County take on water, water heaters leak, and a single afternoon can erase records the IRS says you're supposed to keep for at least three years after filing — and up to seven in some situations. The institutions that issued those documents keep their copies digital and redundant. Yours are one burst pipe away from gone. A filing cabinet is not a backup. It never was.
