## The Home-Shredder Dead End, and Why It Keeps Happening

The thing standing between you and a clean closet is not laziness. It's a system that was never built for you. And it isn't a metaphor — it's a pricing sheet.

Here's how it runs. One path is the $40 desk shredder — rated for a handful of sheets, built for a three-page problem. It jams on staples, overheats after a few dozen pages, and leaves you with a bag of confetti strips you now have to trust to the trash. The other path is the commercial service, and look at what their contracts are built around: pallet minimums, per-stop trip charges, scheduled pickups that run on the office's calendar. Get a quote for two boxes and watch what happens — either the minimum makes your dollar-a-pound problem cost like a records purge, or they hand you off to the locked console behind the strip mall that gets emptied on Thursdays. Think about that console for a second: you drop your mother's medical bills through a slot, and what you get back is a receipt. Not a witness. A receipt. Custody of your paper — and everything that can be reassembled from it — now belongs to a bin and a schedule you're not part of.

That's the whole mechanism: the machine is too small, the contract is too big, and the safe option was priced and packaged for somebody else. You weren't lazy. You were designed out.

And the risk is not theoretical. Identity theft from discarded paper needs no hacking skill at all — a statement plus a piece of junk mail gives a stranger your name, address, account number, and bank. It's common enough that the [FTC maintains a full federal recovery process for identity theft](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/identity-theft).
