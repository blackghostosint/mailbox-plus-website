## The DIY Design Gap

The villain here isn't your printer or your taste. It's the gap between "I can open a template" and "this looks professional."

Template design tools make the first ninety percent of the job feel easy — say, roughly nine-tenths of it, to borrow a round number. Pick a layout, type your name, drop in a phone number. Done, it seems. But the last ten percent — spacing, kerning, color consistency, bleed margins, print resolution — is exactly the part that separates a card people keep from a card people quietly recycle. And that last ten percent is invisible until the cards come back from a home printer with cropped edges and washed-out color.

The gap operates in broad daylight. Nobody tells you your card looks homemade. They just form an impression — a split-second judgment that your business is small, new, or careless — and you never hear about it. The design tools don't warn you. The office supply store doesn't either. It sells you the paper and lets you walk out with the problem.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You're at a networking event or a job site. Someone asks for your card. You hand it over and watch their eyes flick across it — a quarter of a second, but you catch it. You explain your business while they're still deciding whether to keep the card.

Or it's the flyer for your next open house, the one you spent your whole evening on. On screen it looked sharp. Printed, the photo is grainy and the red came out orange. You still hand it out, because you already paid for the paper. You just hand it out slower.

And the hours. Three nights on a logo. Two more on a flyer layout. That's time you didn't bill to anyone — time you stole from the actual work of your business to fight a program you don't really know how to use.

Then there's the print side, which the template tools never mention. Home printers can't bleed ink to the edge of a card, so you get a white sliver where the design was supposed to run full. The file that looks perfect on your monitor exports at a resolution that turns crisp type fuzzy on paper. You reorder paper you don't need, run test sheets you don't want, and still end up at a copy shop counter anyway — with a file that has to be rescued before it can be printed.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Every business, no matter how small, deserves to look professional. Good design shouldn't be a barrier that only companies with a marketing department get past.

A business card is not stationery. It's a first impression you can hand to someone. The judgment it triggers happens before you've said a word — and it should be working for you, not against you.

Professional design was never supposed to be a luxury. It's a standard that small businesses were simply locked out of — by price, by software complexity, by the fact that nobody offers it at the counter of a local shop. Until someone does.
