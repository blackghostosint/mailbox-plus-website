## The Home-Address Default

The villain has a boring name: the home-address default. Marketplaces, suppliers, and shipping platforms all assume the person shipping from a spare room ships _from home_. So the return label they generate shows your residence. The supplier's packing slip shows your residence. A customer who wants a refund can read your street right off the box.

This is not a hack or a leak. It is the system working exactly as designed — for warehouses and storefronts, not for the fastest-growing kind of seller there is: the one working from a kitchen table in Willoughby. The platforms treat your front door as your receiving dock. They have never asked whether you agreed to that trade.

One sale, one label, no problem. But sell three items a week and that is roughly 150 labels a year — 150 copies of your home address circulating in strangers' hands. Somewhere along the way your address stops being something you share and becomes something you _publish_.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You know the moment. The doorbell rings and your stomach drops before you check the camera — is it a neighbor, or a customer? Someone messages the shop at 10 PM asking to "just swing by and return it in person," and you spend twenty minutes composing a reply that doesn't give away your schedule.

So you start making small, quiet adjustments. You drop your city from the shop profile, even though buyers like local sellers. You hesitate before posting photos of your workspace because the window shows the street. You look into a PO Box, then remember the marketplace wants a street address for the carriers that will not deliver to a box number — and the problem settles right back onto your shoulders.

None of this is the work you got into business to do. You wanted to source well, pack well, and keep customers happy. Instead, part of your brain is permanently assigned to managing the risk that your own address created.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

A residence and a business address are two different tools for two different jobs. One protects the people you love. The other receives commerce. The fact that thousands of sellers across Northeast Ohio have to blur those two jobs together — not because they chose to, but because the default shipped that way — is backwards.

Running a business from home should be the _smart_ arrangement, not the risky one. The infrastructure to separate the two already exists, and the only reason sellers keep shipping their home address around Lake County is that nobody told them the separation takes one visit to set up.
