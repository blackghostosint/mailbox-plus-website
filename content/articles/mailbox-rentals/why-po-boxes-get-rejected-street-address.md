## Why "No" Is the Answer So Often

The reason isn't that institutions hate the post office. It's that **a PO box is not an address. It's a locker number at the post office.**

When a bank, the DMV, the IRS, or an insurer asks for your address, they aren't asking where to send mail. They're asking _where you are_ — where you live, where the car sleeps, where the business operates. That's what "address" means in their world. And a PO box gives them none of it:

- **Banks** are federally required to collect a "residential or business street address" for every customer under the USA PATRIOT Act's Customer Identification Program (CIP) rules. Federal examiners and the industry guidance are explicit: a PO box alone is not acceptable as the required address. That's why the online form keeps rejecting you.
- **The DMV** needs proof of where you live. Ohio's own BMV documents say the residence address on your license cannot be a PO box — and the BMV requires two documents from different sources proving an Ohio street address.
- **The IRS** is more flexible than most people think — you can use a PO box as the mailing address on your 1040 in many cases. But when the IRS wants the _business location_ — like the street address line on Form SS-4 when you apply for an EIN — the form says, plainly, "Don't enter a P.O. box."
- **Insurance companies** rate your policy on the car's _garaging address_ — the physical place it's parked at night. A PO box can't be a garaging address, because a PO box isn't a place a car can be.

None of this is a conspiracy. It's the difference between "where do we mail it?" and "where are you?" And that difference is why the same PO box that works for your magazine subscriptions fails for your bank account.

## The 10 Questions, Answered Honestly

Here are the "can you use a PO box for X" questions people actually type — answered straight, with the caveats that matter.

### 1. Can you use a PO box for a driver's license?

**No — not as the residence address.** Ohio's BMV explicitly does not permit a PO box as the residence address on a driver's license, and the BMV's acceptable-documents list requires two documents from different sources proving an _Ohio street address_ for residency. A private mailbox isn't a residence either — no mailbox is, and claiming one as your home would be a problem. What a street-address mailbox _does_ do is give you a reliable, permanent place for the bills, statements, and letters that prove residency — instead of the "PO box? we don't deliver there" dead end.

### 2. Can you use a PO box for car insurance?

**No — not for the garaging address.** Insurance companies base your rate on where the car is parked at night, and that must be a physical location. A PO box cannot be a garaging address because a car can't be parked in one. A private mailbox works the same way here: it can be the mailing address on your policy and a stable place for your documents, but the garaging address has to be real.

### 3. Can you use a PO box for an LLC?

**Partly — and this is where people get burned.** Your LLC can absolutely use a mailing address for business correspondence, and many Ohio LLCs use a mailbox service for exactly that. But Ohio law requires every business to maintain a **statutory agent** — the person who receives legal documents — and the Ohio Secretary of State is explicit: the statutory agent's address must be a street address in Ohio, and _private mailbox services are not allowed_ for that role. So the honest answer: a mailbox can be your business address, but you still need a separate statutory agent at a real street address where someone is present during business hours. Anyone who tells you a PO box or a mailbox store can be your registered agent is wrong.

### 4. Can you use a PO box for taxes?

**Usually yes as a mailing address — but not always.** The IRS instructions for Form 1040 say you enter your PO box if the post office doesn't deliver to your home. But when the IRS needs a physical location — most famously the street-address line on Form SS-4 when you apply for an EIN — a PO box won't work. The instruction is written right on the form: "Don't enter a P.O. box." A street-address mailbox gives you a line you can fill everywhere, without the asterisk.

### 5. Can you use a PO box for a bank account?

**Not as the address the bank requires.** Under federal CIP rules (31 CFR 1020.220), banks must collect a residential or business street address for every account holder. A PO box alone does not satisfy that requirement. A private mailbox _is_ a street address — a real, formatted, CMRA-registered street address — so it generally satisfies the requirement on paper. Be aware that some banks layer their own policies on top of federal rules and may ask for more, so if a bank pushes back, that's their choice, not the law.

### 6. Can you use a PO box for a credit card?

**Same answer as the bank — no, not as the primary address.** Card issuers operate under the same federal CIP rules and want a physical address for verification and fraud protection. A PO box works as a mailing address for statements, but the application will ask for a residential or business street address. A private mailbox fills that role.

### 7. Can you use a PO box for voter registration?

**Not as your home address.** Ohio's voter registration form requires your residence — the address where you actually live — and a PO box is only allowed in the separate, optional "mailing address" field. Same logic applies to a private mailbox: it can be where your voter information gets mailed, but you register to vote where you live.

### 8. Can you use a PO box for a passport?

**Yes, for delivery.** The State Department will generally mail your passport to the mailing address you list on the application, and a PO box can be that address. But the application still asks for your physical address — the place where you live. So a PO box helps with the delivery step, not the identity step.

### 9. Can you use a PO box for a business address — Amazon, eBay, Etsy, licenses?

**Only if the platform accepts it — and many won't.** Online marketplaces and licensing agencies increasingly want a verifiable street address, especially for seller verification and business licenses. A "PO Box" return address gets flagged, delayed, or rejected in verification flows. A private mailbox gives you a formatted street address — 7554 Fredle Drive, Suite (or PMB) 123-style — that passes the same street-address checks a real office address passes.

### 10. Can you use a PO box for UPS, FedEx, or DHL packages?

**No — and this one is absolute.** Only the U.S. Postal Service delivers to PO boxes. UPS, FedEx, and DHL drivers physically cannot put a package in a post office locker. If you ship or receive with any carrier but USPS, the package gets refused, redirected, or left at a facility you'll drive across town to visit. A private mailbox is a street address at a real building, so all four carriers deliver to it — that's the single biggest practical difference between the two.

## What a Private Mailbox Actually Is

A private mailbox (PMB) is not a PO box, and it's not a loophole. It's a specific, regulated thing: a mailbox rented from a **Commercial Mail Receiving Agency (CMRA)** — a business registered with the USPS that accepts mail on behalf of customers. Mailbox Plus is a CMRA. So are The UPS Store locations and thousands of independent mailbox stores.

When you rent a PMB, you get:

- **A real street address.** Your mail is addressed to the store's physical address plus your box number — e.g., 7554 Fredle Drive, #123 — not "P.O. Box 47."
- **All four carriers.** Because it's a street address, UPS, FedEx, DHL, and USPS all deliver.
- **The same federal paperwork as every legitimate mailbox store.** Form 1583, two forms of ID, and a signature. That form is what registers the address with the USPS and makes it a valid street-format address rather than a locker number.

And here's the part that builds the trust this whole article is about: **a private mailbox does not solve everything.** It is not a residence, so it won't fix a driver's license address or voter registration by itself. It is not a statutory agent, so it can't be the registered agent for your Ohio LLC. If someone tells you a mailbox fixes those, they're overselling you — and you deserve better than that.
