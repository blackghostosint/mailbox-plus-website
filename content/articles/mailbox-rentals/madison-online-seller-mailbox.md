## The Home-Address Label

Call the villain what it is: **The Home-Address Label**.

It's not a hacker and not a scam. It's the default. You open a seller account, you type in your address, and from that moment the platform stamps it onto every shipping label, every return label, every packing slip, every carrier manifest. Hundreds of labels over a year, all pointing back to your kitchen table.

The platforms don't hide it — the label generator simply uses the address on file, and for a home-based seller the address on file is your house. the seller help pages at major marketplaces like eBay walk sellers through fixing a wrong return address on labels, which tells you how central the return address is to the flow: it's on the label by design. You'd have to dig through settings, and even then many marketplaces expect a physical return address for every listing.

Now think about who reads that corner of the box. Your buyer. Their family. Their neighbor who signs for the package. The carrier driver. Anyone the box gets misdelivered to. The Federal Trade Commission identifies stolen personal information as the raw material of identity theft — and here's the math that points to: a home address paired with a name is one of the simplest pieces of that material to collect. It arrives gift-wrapped, in person, every time you ship.

The mechanism is legal, ordinary, and almost never questioned. That's what makes it work so well.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Your home is not a business address. It's where your kids sleep, where your mail arrives, where your emergency contacts know to find you. Selling a lamp to a stranger in another state should not hand that stranger the coordinates.

Businesses have always had business addresses for exactly this reason — a public point of contact that is not your private life. Sole proprietors and home sellers get pushed out of that protection not by law but by defaults: the label generator wants an address, so you give it the only one you have.

You shouldn't need a lease on a storefront to get a business address. You shouldn't have to choose between running your little operation and keeping your front door anonymous. The right setup lets you do both at once — sell freely, ship daily, and stay private.

## Why a Street Address Beats a PO Box for a Seller

Here's the honest comparison, because you deserve the whole board:

- **Carrier coverage.** UPS cannot deliver to a PO Box — its own address guidelines say so, warning that shipments addressed to a PO Box face delays, lose service guarantees, and can be hit with an address-correction surcharge. FedEx's shipping FAQ says the only domestic PO Box delivery option is its economy ground service; its main services don't reach a box. DHL's answer to "can I send to a PO Box?" is flat: shipments cannot be delivered to PO boxes. USPS delivers to PO Boxes. A street-address mailbox at our counter takes all four.
- **Marketplace and bank forms.** Some marketplaces, banks, and payment processors flag a PO Box during address verification, and policies vary — a street address clears those forms without a fight.
- **Packages.** A PO Box holds letters and USPS flat pieces. Your candle order from a supplier on UPS Ground can't reach it. Your street-address mailbox does — signed for, held behind the counter, not on your porch.

So if you only ever receive envelopes, a PO Box is a fine tool. The moment you sell physical goods, it's the wrong tool.
