## The Dorm Move-Out

It's the last week of finals and the dorm hallway smells like Febreze and desperation. Your student is shoving a comforter into a garbage bag while you — the parent — hold the cardboard box of things that don't fit anywhere else. The RA is collecting keys. The room has to be empty by 10 AM tomorrow.

And on the desk, there's a stack of mail that arrived this week: the bank card that was reissued after the old one expired, the voter registration confirmation, the internship offer letter with a deadline, and a package slip for the care package your sister sent three weeks ago.

The address on all of it? The dorm. The room that will not exist tomorrow.

So you take the mail. You'll forward it somehow. Next semester the student gets an apartment, a different room number, a different mailbox situation. And the cycle starts over.

Here's the question nobody asks until the third time it happens: **why does your student's address have to change every single year?**

## The Address Problem No One Warns You About

When your kid goes to college, you think through tuition, housing, meal plans, and books. Nobody hands you a pamphlet about the mail. But mail is the quiet, relentless way that real life reaches your student:

- The **bank** sends a new debit card when the old one expires.
- The **DMV** sends a renewal notice for a license that expires during sophomore year.
- The **county board of elections** sends a voter registration confirmation — and, in an election year, a ballot.
- The **court** sends a jury duty summons.
- The **internship** sends an offer letter, a background-check packet, and later a W-2.
- The **government** sends tax documents, FAFSA correspondence, and a Selective Service registration letter.
- **Grandma** sends a birthday card with cash in it.

Every one of those pieces of mail is addressed to wherever your student said they live at that moment. And in college, that address changes like a rental car:

- Freshman year: dorm room 214, which closes in May.
- Sophomore year: an apartment with three roommates and a mailbox that's a slot in a door.
- Junior year: a different apartment, because the landlord sold the building.
- Senior year: another place, because the lease ended.
- Then: the first job, in a city you may not even know yet.

Each move means updating the bank, the DMV, the IRS, the university, the voter rolls, the streaming subscriptions — and hoping nothing falls through the cracks in between. Something always does. The reissued card goes to the old address. The internship offer lands after the deadline. The ballot gets returned because the name on the mailbox didn't match.

That's not a parenting failure. It's a system failure — and it has a fix.

## What a Stable Mailing Address Actually Is

A private mailbox (PMB) is a real street address you rent — not from the post office, but from a licensed mail receiving business like Mailbox Plus. It is not a PO box. It's a specific, registered thing: when you rent one, you fill out the standard USPS **Form 1583** (two forms of ID, a signature, about ten minutes), and your mail is addressed to the store's street address plus your box number.

For a college student, that means one address that doesn't change when the dorm closes, the apartment lease ends, or the first job starts:

**7554 Fredle Drive, Suite (or PMB) #\_\_\_\_, Concord Township, OH 44077**

That address works for all four years. And beyond.

Because it's a street address — not a "P.O. Box 47" — **all four carriers deliver to it**: USPS, UPS, FedEx, and DHL. That single fact matters more for a college student than for almost anyone else, because half the packages in a student's life come from a parent, and parents ship with whatever carrier is cheapest that week. A PO box cannot receive a single FedEx, UPS, or DHL package — the driver physically can't put it in a post office locker. A private mailbox can receive all of them, signed for by real people who hold it until the student walks in.

## What the Mailbox Saves You (and Your Student)

Walk through the four years with a stable address and the difference is obvious:

**Voter registration.** Ohio's voter registration form requires a residence address — the place where the student actually lives — and a private mailbox can't be that. But the form has a separate, optional **mailing address** field, and that's where the mailbox shines. The student registers to vote where they live, and every confirmation, sample ballot, and official notice gets mailed to the address that never changes. No more "my ballot went to my old dorm."

**Bank cards and financial mail.** Banks are federally required to collect a street address for every customer (the Customer Identification Program rules under the Patriot Act — a PO box alone doesn't satisfy it). A private mailbox is a street address, which is what the bank form actually wants. And when the new debit card or the statement arrives, it lands at a counter where a human holds it — not in a slot that a roommate's guest can walk past.

**Driver's license and ID.** The DMV needs a residence address on the license, and no mailbox — private or post office — can be that. But the paperwork that _proves_ residency — the bills, the bank statements, the official letters — needs a reliable place to arrive. And the renewal notice that would otherwise go to last year's apartment now goes somewhere the student (or you) can actually pick it up.

**Jury duty and court mail.** Jury summonses are mailed. Court notices are mailed. They don't wait for you to update your address, and they don't get resent just because you moved. A stable mailing address means that when the county sends something that has a deadline, it goes somewhere reachable.

**Internships and the first job.** Offer letters, background check packets, offer acceptance forms, W-2s, and 1099s all get mailed. An internship offer that sits in a dead mailbox for two weeks is an internship that got filled by someone else. The summer between junior and senior year is exactly when the mailbox earns its keep.

**Care packages.** This one is pure joy: the package with the homemade cookies, the birthday gift, the forgotten sweatshirt. When a parent ships from home, they don't want to ask "which address is it this semester?" They want to ship to the one address that works — and know that if the student is in class, a staff member signs for it and it waits safely in a locked building.

## Honest Limits (Because We Don't Oversell)

A private mailbox is not a magic wand, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something. It is not a residence, so it can't be the residence address on a driver's license or voter registration. It doesn't replace the university registrar. It won't make the student's mail appear faster.

What it does is solve the actual problem: **the address that keeps changing**. The residence will change — that's normal and fine. The mailbox is the constant. It's the one address you can put on a form in 2026 and still use in 2030.
