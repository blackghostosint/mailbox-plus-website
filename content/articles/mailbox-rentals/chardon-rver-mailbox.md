## The Tether

This isn’t just a logistical hiccup; it’s a systemic problem, a silent tyranny we call "The Tether." It’s the ingrained, almost invisible assumption that to exist fully in the modern world, you must be physically anchored to a stationary building. A house, an apartment, a brick-and-mortar address that doesn't move. This isn't your fault, it's how the system is built, a relic from an era before wheels could be homes and the internet could be your office. The Tether operates like an invisible fence, allowing you to roam only so far before yanking you back, reminding you of your perceived immobility.

Consider the mechanisms of this tether. Banks, those bastions of financial stability, demand a physical address for opening accounts, receiving statements, and even applying for loans. Try explaining your "home is where I park it" philosophy to a loan officer. Insurance companies, whether for your RV, your health, or your car, rely on a static address to calculate risk and send vital policy documents. Without one, coverage can be denied, premiums can skyrocket, or worse, you could find yourself uninsured when you need it most.

Then there’s voting. Our fundamental right, inextricably linked to a physical address. You want to participate in the civic life of Geauga County, to cast your ballot in local and national elections, but the system demands a residence. And healthcare – doctor’s offices, hospitals, pharmacies – they all need a definitive, traceable location for records, billing, and prescriptions. Imagine needing urgent medical care miles from Chardon, only to find your records are inaccessible because your address is in flux. This isn't a personal failing on your part for choosing a mobile lifestyle; it's a foundational flaw in how essential services are delivered, designed for a world that no longer fully exists. This "blackout," this systemic exclusion, happens in plain legal daylight, simply because your home doesn't have a foundation.
