## The Tether

The unseen force holding you back from absolute, unburdened freedom isn't a physical chain, nor is it a person. It’s a concept, a silent assumption that has woven itself into the fabric of modern society. We call it **The Tether**.

The Tether is the pervasive, often unstated, assumption that to exist fully in the world, you _must_ have a physical, permanent, stick-and-brick building as your address. It’s the invisible cord that connects your entire identity – your financial life, your legal standing, your healthcare access, your voting rights – to a static geographic point. This isn't a personal failing on your part; it's a systemic, deeply ingrained mechanism that operates in plain sight, yet often remains unquestioned.

Consider it a "blackout" or a "franchise monopoly" over your physical location. Banks, those bastions of financial stability, demand a physical street address for opening accounts, securing loans, or even receiving vital statements. Insurance companies, offering you protection against life's uncertainties, will often balk at anything other than a fixed residence for policies. Voting, the fundamental right of every citizen, hinges on you being registered at a verifiable, permanent address. Healthcare providers, from your family doctor in Chardon to specialists you might see while traveling, need a consistent address for billing, records, and mailing prescriptions. Even your driver's license and vehicle registration are inextricably linked to a "home" address.

Without one, you don't just lose access; you become administratively invisible. You cease to be a "resident" in the eyes of these critical institutions. You might be a conscientious tax-paying citizen, a responsible individual, an active member of society, but if your address is fluid, shifting from a home in Chardon to a campground in Myrtle Beach, then to an RV park in Tucson, the system struggles to categorize you.

The Tether forces you into an endless game of administrative gymnastics. You use a friend’s address, feeling guilty for burdening them. You send mail to a relative, hoping they remember to check it. You try to use general delivery at a local post office, only to find it's often unreliable, confusing, and limited. The system, designed for a stationary populace of yesteryear, views your mobile lifestyle not as a choice, but as a problem. It’s a frustrating, often infuriating, mechanism that constantly reminds you that your freedom, while exhilarating, comes with an often-unbearable administrative cost. This isn't just about mail; it's about your ability to function as a fully recognized individual in the modern world.
