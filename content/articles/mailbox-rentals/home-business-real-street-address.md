## Why the Forms Insist on a Street Address

It is not one picky form. You are at the kitchen table with the bank application open, the cursor blinking in the physical-address field, your home address already half-typed — and the form wants more than that. Four different institutions require a real location, each for its own published reason, so this field will keep appearing no matter which application you fill out.

**Banks.** Under the federal Customer Identification Program rule — 31 CFR 1020.220, part of the USA PATRIOT Act framework — a bank must collect, at minimum, a residential or business street address for an individual customer, and for a company, a principal place of business, local office, or other physical location. The rule is about knowing who they are serving; a box number inside a post office does not satisfy it. When the business-account application says "physical address," that is the regulation talking.

**Ohio LLC filings.** Ohio's Articles of Organization (Form 610 from the Secretary of State, $99 filing fee) states it plainly: post office boxes and CMRA addresses are **not allowed** for the statutory agent address. The statutory agent is the person or company legally designated to accept lawsuits and official documents for your LLC, and Ohio wants a place where someone can actually be served during normal hours.

**Google Business Profile.** Google's published guidelines are direct: "P.O. boxes or mailboxes located at remote locations aren't acceptable." Google also states that a rented mailing address you do not operate from — a virtual office — isn't eligible for a Business Profile. The profile exists to show customers where a business actually is.

**Vendor and supplier forms.** Wholesale applications, trade-credit applications, and partner registrations routinely require a physical address, and many spell out "no PO box" right in the field. A company deciding whether to extend credit reads a PO box as "no fixed location."

Add it up: the PO box is the first thing most home businesses try, and it fails the bank, the state, Google, and the vendor form almost in a row. That is not bad luck. It is four institutions independently wanting the same thing — a physical place.

## The Problem Nobody Warns You About: It's Your Home

When every street-address field gets your home address, your home starts showing up in places you never chose to put it:

- **Bank records** — the business account, the debit card, the statements, the merchant processor.
- **Vendor databases** — every supplier, platform, and registration stores it, and those lists get merged, sold, and breached.
- **Public filings** — an LLC's records show street addresses, and for a home business that is the owner's house.
- **Google Maps** — a Business Profile with a home address can put your front door on the map for anyone who searches your business name.

None of it feels dangerous the day you type it in. It compounds for years: a customer who wants to "stop by" knows exactly where you live, and data brokers connect your business name to your house and resell the pairing. The false choice most owners feel stuck with — rent an office you don't need, or expose your home — is not actually a choice the rules force on you. There is a third option, and it is the verdict at the top of this page.

## The Honest Boundaries (Read This Before You Switch)

A guide that only sells is a guide that sets you up to be blindsided. A private mailbox does **not** fill two slots, and both have clean answers:

**Ohio's statutory agent slot.** Form 610 is explicit: post office boxes and CMRA addresses are not allowed for the statutory agent. If you do not want your home on that line, the clean answer is a professional registered agent service — a real office that accepts legal documents for you. The division of labor: your PMB is the address the _world_ sees (bank, vendors, website, cards); the statutory agent is the address the _courthouse_ uses. Different jobs, and a mailbox only does one of them.

**Google Business Profile's location rule.** Google will not accept a PO box, a remote mailbox, or a rented address you do not operate from as a profile location. If your business serves customers at their locations — consulting, contracting, mobile services — set the profile up as a service-area business and hide the address; that is Google's own sanctioned path for a business run from a residential address. The PMB remains the address on your website, cards, and registrations; the profile rule is a separate requirement, not a rejection of your setup.
