## A Small Job With a Hidden Tax

You list it, you sell it, you pack it, you ship it. Somewhere between the second and third step, the job picks up a tax you never agreed to: every label you print puts your home address in front of a stranger, every inbound return needs someone standing where the driver shows up, and every delivery you can't intercept sits on your porch while you're at work or running errands.

It should work like the rest of your business: a fixed place, a fixed process, no improvising. That's what a package receiving service is — a staffed street address where every carrier can deliver, someone puts your packages behind a counter, and your home address never has to ride on a label again.

## The Doorstep Drop: The Mechanism at Work

The problem isn't that carriers are careless. It's that residential delivery is designed around the assumption that someone is home. That assumption fails constantly for anyone who sells from home and works somewhere else — which is most eBay sellers.

So the system builds in failure modes, and each one lands on you: a box baking in the sun on concrete while its delivery confirmation says it arrived an hour ago, a door tag flapping where the package should be, a label with your name and street on it riding off in a stranger's mailbox photo:

- **The unattended drop.** Drivers on residential routes leave packages at the door because stopping to get a signature costs time. If nobody signs, the package sits in the open — a public, visible, empty delivery point.
- **The missed-attempt spiral.** When a carrier does require a signature, they attempt once. Miss it, and the package goes to a depot across town with pickup hours that don't fit a workday. Some sellers drive across town to retrieve an item they shipped in the first place.
- **The address leak.** Your return label is the mechanism. It's printed on every outbound package, visible to every buyer who handles the box, and tied to maps and people-search sites in a few clicks. A PO Box would fix the leak — except UPS, FedEx, Amazon, and other private carriers cannot deliver to a PO Box, per [USPS's own guidance](/articles/private-mailbox-vs-po-box/).

Notice the structure: none of these are your mistakes. You're using the postal system the way it's built. The system just wasn't built for a home-based seller, and the cost of that mismatch shows up as your stolen packages, your second trips, and your address on a stranger's screen.

## What the Friction Costs in Real Terms

Run the actual numbers on one lost package. You refund the buyer — the item price plus the postage they paid toward a house nobody was watching. You're out the item, the postage, and the listing momentum, and you spend your evening filing a claim instead of listing the next item.

Then there's the steady leak: the buyer who asked where you're located and got quiet when the answer was a residential street. The neighbor who signed for your box and couldn't find it for a week. The vacation where you had a relative collect your porch because an unattended package during an announced absence is a beacon. Each one is small. Together they're the reason selling from home feels like running a shop whose front door can't close.

The buyer who Googles your street and sees a ranch house with a tricycle in the yard doesn't need to say anything. The address already did.

Your driver isn't paid to wait for you. He's paid to clear the route by six. So he drops your box on the concrete and keeps moving — the system rewards him for your risk. A secure handoff costs them minutes, a porch drop costs them nothing — so you eat the risk and they keep the margin. UPS and FedEx have no financial reason to make a porch drop secure for free — and no mechanism to know whether you're home. The Post Office solves half the problem with the PO Box and then, by rule, excludes the carriers that bring most of your eBay inventory in and out.

So the market leaves a gap: a fixed, staffed, secure delivery point that accepts every carrier and signs for the ones that need it. That gap is exactly what a commercial mail receiving agency fills. That's why the address works like a real storefront — and why the only paperwork is a single page we keep right at the counter, no government alphabet soup required.
