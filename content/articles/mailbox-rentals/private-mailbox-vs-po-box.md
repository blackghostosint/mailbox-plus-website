## The Verdict First

Straight answer, no suspense: **if your mail is letters only — bills, cards, magazines — and it always will be, a PO Box does the job.** It is inexpensive, it is private, and it is run by the Post Office itself.

**If anything you receive ever ships via FedEx, UPS, or DHL — and for a business, something always does — a private mailbox wins, and it is not close.** A PO Box physically cannot accept those carriers' packages. A private mailbox gives you a real street address that all of them deliver to.

That is the whole decision. The rest of this page is the detail behind it — what each option actually costs you, and where each one breaks.

## The One Fact That Decides Everything

A PO Box is a numbered compartment inside a post office lobby. The United States Postal Service delivers to it. That is the complete list of who delivers to it.

FedEx drivers do not have keys to the post office lobby. UPS does not route packages to box numbers. DHL does not sort by compartment. When a seller or supplier ships your order through any of those carriers and the label says "PO Box 47," the package is not delayed — it is undeliverable. It goes back to the carrier's facility, and you find out by chasing a tracking page that says "delivery exception."

A private mailbox — what the postal system calls a CMRA address, a Commercial Mail Receiving Agency — is a counter with a real street address. Every carrier delivers to it because every carrier delivers to street addresses. One address covers all four carriers — UPS, FedEx, USPS, and DHL — no exceptions. All four pick up and deliver to our counter, and we're an authorized shipper for each.

That is the entire comparison in one paragraph. The rest is what it costs, what it unlocks, and the one case where the PO Box still makes sense.

## Where the PO Box Still Wins

This is the part most comparisons skip, and skipping it makes the comparison dishonest.

A PO Box is the right answer if all of these are true: you receive letters only, you do not run a business that receives packages, and you want the cheapest private mail point that exists. The Post Office runs it, the price is low, and for a retiree who gets bills and birthday cards, it works.

The trap is not choosing a PO Box. The trap is choosing a PO Box for a business — or for a household with an online shopping habit — and finding out about the carrier wall after the first undeliverable package. If you are reading a comparison article, there is a decent chance you are already past that point.

## What a Private Mailbox Actually Unlocks

The street address is not just a package receiver. Because a private mailbox is a real street address backed by a registered CMRA, it changes what your address can _do_:

**It survives carrier diversity.** Vendors ship with whoever is cheapest. Customers return with whatever label the marketplace generates. Suppliers switch carriers mid-season. A street address absorbs all of it without you ever thinking about it.

**It keeps your home address out of the record.** Every shipping label, vendor invoice, and public filing carries your address. With a home address, that is your house — on boxes, on forms, on storefronts across the internet. A private mailbox is a real address that is not your front door.

**It reads as a business.** "Suite" and "Studio" numbers on a street address tell customers and vendors what a box number cannot: there is a staffed counter here, a sign on the building, someone who signed for the delivery. Whether that matters for credibility is your call, but the difference is real.

**It comes with a person.** Packages are logged when they arrive. If you are on vacation, they are held. If something needs forwarding, it gets forwarded. A slot in a wall does none of that.

One honest caveat: because a CMRA address is not your residential address, a few institutions have their own rules about what they will accept it for — banks and government agencies vary, and the question to ask any of them is simply "do you accept a CMRA street address?" We will tell you honestly what our counter address can and cannot be used for before you set anything up. (For the specific case of bank and DMV rejections, see [why PO Boxes get rejected](/articles/why-po-boxes-get-rejected-street-address/).)

## Switching Is a Form, Not a Project

If you already have a PO Box, the switch is not a migration. It is a form.

Federal law requires a signed USPS Form 1583 to authorize a CMRA to receive your mail — that is the one piece of paperwork, and it exists to protect you: no one can start receiving your mail without proving identity. Bring two forms of ID (a driver's license plus a second document works), sign the form at our counter, and the address is live.

You keep your PO Box until the switch is done, tell the important senders your new street address — and since the new address accepts every carrier, this is the last time you will ever do it.

A PO Box is cheaper. We will not pretend otherwise, and anyone who does is selling you something.
