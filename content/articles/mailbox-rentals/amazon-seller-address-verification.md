## The Postcard That Lands on Your Doorstep

It starts with a small white postcard in your mailbox. On it, a six-digit code. No marketing copy, no coupon — just a code Amazon wants you to type into Seller Central so it knows your business address is real.

For most sellers, that postcard is a five-minute inconvenience. For a growing number of sellers, it's the moment they realize the address on file with Amazon is their **home** — and Amazon has just confirmed it, on paper, in the mail.

The address you registered with Seller Central isn't just a field in a form. It's the address Amazon verifies. It's the address that gets printed on that postcard. And in many cases, it's the address any customer can see on your public seller profile. That's the part nobody reads until the postcard arrives.

This article is the honest version of what passes Amazon's address verification, what fails it, and how to make sure your home address is never the thing being verified.

## Why Amazon Verifies Seller Addresses at All

Amazon hasn't always mailed postcards. It started in earnest in 2021 — the company told CNBC it was sending postcards to third-party sellers to confirm "the business address displayed on your Amazon.com seller profile page" was authentic. The stated goal was to keep counterfeiters and bad actors off the marketplace, and the mechanics are simple: Amazon mails a postcard with a verification code, the seller enters the code in Seller Central, and Amazon knows the address is real and reachable.

Then the rules got legal teeth.

In 2023, the **INFORM Consumers Act** took effect (June 27, 2023). Under that federal law, Amazon must collect, verify, and periodically re-verify specific information about "high-volume" sellers — defined as sellers who've sold 200 or more new or unused consumer products **and** had at least $5,000 in gross revenues in a continuous 12-month period during the previous 24 months. The information Amazon must verify includes your name, a government-issued ID, a working email and phone number, your bank account information, your tax ID — and your **business address**.

Here's what that means in practice: if you're an FBA seller, a merchant-fulfilled seller, or anyone moving real volume, Amazon is legally required to confirm your address. And it has to re-verify that information whenever it changes, plus ask high-volume sellers to certify it's still current at least once a year. The verification window is real. Miss the deadlines and Amazon can deactivate the account — and, per its own guidance and the CNBC reporting on the postcard program, it can withhold disbursements for sellers who don't verify.

## What Amazon Actually Accepts

When Amazon asks you to verify your business address, the official Seller Central help page ("Address verification") walks through the steps:

1. From **Account Information**, click the verification action items that are pending.
2. If your current business address is incorrect, select a different address or add a new one.
3. Upload a **proof of address** document.
4. Click **Save and continue**.

The proof-of-address documents Amazon accepts are specific. For both individual and professional sellers, the official list includes:

- A bank statement (checking, savings, or loan account)
- A credit card statement or bill
- A certified bank letter
- A utility bill — gas, water, electricity, TV, internet, mobile, or landline phone
- A rent or lease agreement
- A mortgage statement
- A tax identity document that contains your address
- For business sellers: a business license or business extract

And the documents have to meet strict criteria:

- Issued within the **last 180 days**
- Show an issuing date, due date, or transaction dates
- Show the issuing institution's name, stamp, signature, or logo
- Show the **name and complete address of the account holder** — which must match what you entered in Seller Central

Read that last requirement again, because it's the whole game: the document must show **your** name and **your** complete address, and it has to match the address on your Amazon account. A utility bill in your spouse's name doesn't pass. A bank statement that still shows your old apartment doesn't pass. A document issued 8 months ago doesn't pass.

## What Fails the Test

Now the part most sellers learn the hard way: **a PO box doesn't pass.**

Amazon's own address-entry guidelines in the verification flow flag PO boxes and remote locations as not acceptable for the business address. And even setting the guidelines aside, look at the document list — the proof of address has to be a utility bill, a bank statement, or a lease showing your name and a complete address. A PO box doesn't receive utility bills. Your bank doesn't mail statements to a PO box in most cases. There's no lease on a PO box. The documents that pass Amazon's verification simply don't exist for a PO box.

The seller forums are full of the aftermath. Long-time sellers who registered their business at a PO box — sometimes for a decade or more, with the IRS and their EIN tied to it — hit the INFORM re-verification wave and found themselves staring at deactivation banners, chasing bank statements that matched, and hoping a postcard sent to a PO box would clear the queue. One thread sums up the feeling: "I would prefer to not utilize my home address as my business address." It's the same panic, over and over: the address that passes isn't the address they want public.

Here's what fails Amazon's verification, in one list:

- **A PO box** — flagged in the address-entry guidelines; no acceptable proof-of-address document exists for one
- **An address that doesn't match your documents** — the bank statement says one thing, Seller Central says another
- **A virtual-only address with no physical presence** — Amazon wants an address that can receive a postcard and produce documents
- **Documents older than 180 days** — the date rule is a hard cutoff

And here's the trap: the address that passes every test — your **home** — is also the address you probably don't want in Amazon's system.

## The Public Part Nobody Reads

Here's the fact that changes the calculation.

Since **September 1, 2020**, Amazon has displayed a seller's business name and address on their public **Seller Profile** page — the page any customer can open by clicking your store name. For individual sellers, Amazon displays the individual's name and address. Amazon's own announcement said it plainly: "Beginning on September 1, 2020, we will display a seller's business name and address on their Amazon.com Seller Profile page."

The INFORM Act made the same disclosure a legal requirement. Amazon's official help page on the act states: "Once verified, certain details including your business name, address, and contact information are displayed on your seller profile page so customers can identify who they're buying from." An Amazon staff member on the seller forums put it even more directly: **for business accounts, the registered business address is typically what appears in the seller's public information.**

Read that with your own account in mind. The address you registered — the one that passes verification, the one that produces a utility bill — is the address a customer can find on your seller profile. If that address is your home, then your home is part of your public storefront. Every competitor, every disgruntled buyer, every curious stranger with your seller name can see where you live.

This isn't hypothetical. It's the exact reason the r/FulfillmentByAmazon threads about "prefer to keep my home address private" exist. FBA sellers — even ones whose products never touch their own hands — still have a Seller Central account with a registered business address, and that address is the one Amazon verifies, re-verifies, and can display.
