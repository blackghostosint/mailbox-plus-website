## The Default That Publishes Your Street

Call it what it is: the home-address default. It isn't a hacker, a scam, or a mistake you made. It's the system working exactly as designed — for warehouses and storefronts, not for the kitchen-table seller.

When you open a seller account, you type in the only address you have: your house. From that moment, the platform stamps it onto every shipping label, every return label, every packing slip. eBay's own seller guide for return shipping walks you through the step of confirming your return address on the label before it goes to the buyer — that's how central your address is to the flow. On Etsy, the shipping profile asks for your origin zip and address, and the label prints from what you have on file. Neither platform is malicious. They simply assume the address you sell from is the address you want published. For a home seller, that assumption is the whole problem.

Now think about who reads that corner of the box. Your buyer. Their roommate who signs for the package. The carrier driver. Whoever gets the box misdelivered next door. The Federal Trade Commission treats stolen personal information as the raw material of identity theft, and a name paired with a home address is one of the simplest pieces of that material to collect — arriving gift-wrapped, in person, every time you ship.

One label is an oddity. Sell a few items a week and that's roughly 150 labels a year — 150 copies of your home address circulating in strangers' hands. At some point you stop sharing your address and start publishing it.

## Two Different Addresses for Two Different Jobs

Your home is where your family is. It is not a receiving dock, a returns department, or a public reception desk for your shop.

Businesses have always had business addresses for exactly this reason — a public point of contact that isn't your private life. Sole proprietors and home sellers get pushed out of that protection not by law but by defaults: the label generator wants an address, so you feed it the only one you have. The system was built assuming sellers have storefronts. Most don't. And the platforms offer no simple built-in way to separate the two unless you want to pay for a warehouse you don't need.

You shouldn't have to choose between running your shop and keeping your front door anonymous. The right setup lets you do both at once — sell freely, ship daily, and stay private. That separation has always been one decision away. It just takes an actual business address.

Now, credit where due: a PO Box is a fine tool for what it does. For plain letter mail at a post office, it's cheap and reliable, and USPS even offers a street-addressing option at participating locations. The failure is packages. UPS states plainly in its own address guidelines that it cannot deliver to a PO Box — and a shipment addressed to one can face delays, lose service guarantees, and draw an address-correction surcharge. FedEx's Ground Economy routes PO Box destinations through USPS for final-mile delivery; its main services don't reach a box. DHL's answer on the subject is flat: shipments cannot be delivered to PO boxes. The moment a return moves on a private carrier — which is exactly how most marketplace returns move — the box number fails. A street-address mailbox takes all four carriers.
