## The Delivery You Can't Supervise

You didn't choose for the box to land on your porch at 1:40 in the afternoon. The carrier chose that. You chose to order the thing — the replacement part, the birthday gift, the inventory your small business is waiting on. The delivery window the app showed you was six hours wide. Nobody is home for six hours. So the box sits on the front step, in full view of the street, until your car pulls in after five.

You already know how that math ends. Some days it ends fine. Some days the tracking page says "delivered" and the porch is empty.

The fix is not a camera, a doorbell, or a note taped to the door. The fix is getting your packages off your porch entirely — received by a person, held behind a counter, handed to you with an ID check. ## The Open Porch Problem

Call the mechanism what it is: the unattended drop-off. The last mile of your delivery ends at a spot nobody is guarding — a doormat, a screen door, a bench by the garage. The driver photographs the box, marks the stop complete, and moves on. From that second until you get home, your package's security is a public honor system. Anyone who walks up the driveway can end it.

This is not a small leak. The U.S. Postal Service Office of Inspector General studied last-mile package theft in 2025 and put a number on it: **at least 58 million packages were stolen in the United States in 2024**. The OIG's white paper describes porch piracy as "a significant challenge for the entire parcel delivery industry," one that imposes real financial costs on consumers, retailers, and carriers alike. Those are the government's words, not ours.

The unattended drop-off keeps operating because it is cheap for everyone except you. It costs the carrier nothing to leave a box at your door. It costs the retailer nothing if the tracking page says delivered. When the package vanishes, the loss — the reordering, the refund fight, the weeks of waiting — lands on your side of the glass.

Ohio decided the honor system deserved worse than shame. Under [Ohio Revised Code 2913.021](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2913.021), taking a package off someone's porch, out of a mailbox, or from a loading dock is now theft of mail or a delivery — a felony of the fifth degree. Law enforcement across the state, including campus police at Ohio State, have publicized the change since it took effect in 2025. The penalty got heavier. The porch did not get safer. A law that fires after the box is gone does nothing for the gift that was supposed to arrive Thursday.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You know the routine. The tracking app is open before you even sit down in the car. "Delivered 1:37 PM — front porch." It's 3:10, you're stuck at work or in a school pickup line, and the box is just sitting there. You are twelve miles away in an office on Mentor Avenue, and every check of your phone tells you the same thing: the box is still out there. There is nothing to do except drive home faster.

Then the version that hurts worse: you get home, the driveway is empty, and the tracking photo shows a box propped against a door that isn't yours. Now you're the detective. You call the retailer, who points you to the carrier. The carrier points you to a claims form. You file it, wait, and reorder the thing you needed — paying twice for one order, or fighting a seller who insists the photo proves delivery. You did nothing wrong. You still did all the work.

And if you run a business out of your house, multiply the shame. Your customer emails asking where her order is, and the only answer you have is 'the carrier says it was delivered' — which sounds exactly like what a hobbyist says before he refunds her out of pocket. One stolen supplier shipment and your fulfillment day collapses while a stranger on your street is opening your inventory.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Nobody would design delivery this way on purpose. If a bank left your cash on the front step and asked you to trust the neighborhood, you'd change banks. A package is money — sometimes fifty dollars, sometimes five hundred, sometimes the only copy of something you can't easily replace. The fact that leaving it unattended is the industry's default says everything about whose time the system optimizes. It is optimized for the route, not for you.

The counterargument you'll hear: "Just get a camera." A camera records the theft. It does not prevent it. The police will accept the footage as evidence of a felony under Ohio's new law, which is progress of a kind — but evidence of a crime is not the same as not being the victim of one. Prevention means the box is never on the porch in the first place. That's it. That's the whole standard.
