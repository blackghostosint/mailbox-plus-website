## The Scene That Starts Everything

You filed your LLC with the Ohio Secretary of State last week. You did everything right — the Articles of Organization, the Operating Agreement, the initial filing fee. But when you look at the public filing on the Secretary of State's website, there it is: your home address.

Your street number. Your street name. Your personal residence. Published in a public database that anyone can search — lawyers, marketers, process servers, and anyone else with an internet connection.

You did not know it would be public. The form asked for a "principal office address," and since you run your business from your home office on Maple Street in Concord Township, you put your home address. The form went through. Your LLC is official. And now your home is in the public record.

This is not a mistake on your part. The Ohio Secretary of State requires a physical address for the principal office — a PO Box is not accepted. And if you work from home, your home address is your physical address.

But the consequences of publishing your home address go beyond privacy concerns. Vendors run commercial credit checks that appear at your residence. Customer inquiries show your home on Google Maps. Legal notices and service of process arrive at your front door.

Your home is supposed to be your sanctuary. But for your business, it is now also a public office.

## The Ohio Public Address Problem

The requirement is clear under Ohio law: every business entity registered with the Secretary of State must provide a "principal office address." This address becomes part of the public record. It appears in the Ohio Business Search database, which is searchable by anyone.

For businesses that operate out of a commercial space — a retail storefront, an office building, a shared workspace — this is not an issue. The business address is already public and separate from the owner's home.

But for home-based businesses — and in Concord Township and the surrounding Lake County suburbs, a significant portion of small businesses are home-based — the requirement creates a dilemma. You either publish your home address in a public registry, or you do not register your business at all.

And a PO Box will not work. The Ohio Secretary of State explicitly requires a physical street address. A numbered postal box does not qualify.

So home-based business owners choose between privacy and compliance. You should not have to make that choice.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

The state requires a street address for a legitimate reason: it needs a physical location where legal documents can be served and official correspondence can be received. The requirement exists for transparency and due process.

But that requirement was designed for businesses with physical commercial locations. It was not designed for the independent consultant, the freelance designer, the home-based baker, or the online retailer operating out of a spare bedroom.

The system treats all businesses the same. A fifty-person law firm gets the same address requirement as a one-person Etsy shop. The requirement makes sense for the law firm. It creates problems for the Etsy shop.

A business address should protect your privacy, not expose it.
