## The Canceled Fiber Bill That Starts a 21-Day Clock

You finally canceled your AT&T fiber. Good for you — that was a ten-minute phone call and a relief. Then, in the confirmation email, two sentences: "Return your equipment within 21 days. A non-return fee will apply."

Now the pile sits on your dining room table. A white gateway the size of a thick paperback. A Wi-Fi extender. Two power supplies — one gray, one black — and you are not sure which device each one belongs to. And a nine-digit account number you had to dig out of an old bill.

Somewhere, a clock is running. You have three weeks to figure out where this equipment goes, how to package it, and how to prove you sent it back — or AT&T adds $150 to your final bill. Not a fine. Not a fee on the way in. Just a charge that appears because you missed a window most people never knew existed.

## The Return Hiding in Fine Print

Here is the thing about returning AT&T equipment: it is not hard, but it is genuinely easy to get wrong, and AT&T does not make the rules obvious.

The equipment has to go back — not because it is valuable to you, but because AT&T will bill you for it if it does not. The amounts are blunt: $150 for a Wi-Fi or All-Fi gateway, $200 for an All-Fi Hub, $65 for a Wi-Fi extender or All-Fi booster that came with your extended Wi-Fi service. Return it damaged from rough handling and there can be a damaged-equipment fee on top. The policy is written in dollars because the dollars are the enforcement.

There are also small traps in the details. Wall-mounted gear — the fiber jack, the Optical Network Terminal, anything a technician screwed into the wall — stays put. Do not unplug it, do not remove it. And there is a strict list of what has to come back: the hub, the extender, and the power supply for each. The HDMI cables, the ethernet cables, and the extra extender you bought yourself? Those are yours to keep — we break the whole list down in [our guide to returning AT&T Fiber equipment and cables](/articles/att-fiber-equipment-return-cables/).

None of this arrives in a letter. It is scattered across a support page, a confirmation email, and the memory of a phone call you half-listened to. Miss the 21-day mark and the fee appears on your next bill — and you are on the phone for an hour trying to reverse it.

## Staring at a Gateway You Are Not Sure You Can Return

It is the ambiguity that gets you. You stand at the table and ask the same questions out loud: Do I have to box this up? Do I need a label? Do I take it to an AT&T store? Was there a return kit I was supposed to get?

Every wrong answer has a cost. Take it to the wrong place and the clerk says no. Send the wrong thing and you still owe the fee. Miss the window and you are paying $150 for a router you stopped using months ago.

So it sits. The dust films over the gateway's vents. The sticky note with your account number curls off the table edge and drops behind the mail pile — twice. You nudge the power cords aside with your coffee mug every morning, and every morning the mug leaves a new ring on the receipt you have not filed. The clock does not care that you are busy.

None of that should be complicated. A router you no longer even want should never become a $200 hostage. The process itself is genuinely simple once you know the two or three things that actually matter — and most of the fear is just not knowing them yet.

## How the AT&T Return Actually Works

Here is exactly what the rep behind the FedEx counter will ask for.

**What has to go back.** The All-Fi Hub or gateway — the white unit. The exact model number depends on your plan: the BGW320, BGW210, and CGW450 are the All-Fi Hub models, while the BGW830 and BGW530 appear on AT&T Internet Air. Any Wi-Fi extenders that came as part of your plan — the AirTies 4971, 4981, or 4991. And the power supply for each piece. That is it.

**What stays.** Anything mounted to the wall — the fiber jack, the ONT, the green cable — leave it in place. Do not unplug it. And cables, third-party extenders, and gear you bought yourself are yours to keep.

**Where it goes.** AT&T accepts returns at a company-owned FedEx Office or a The UPS Store. You do not box it, you do not print a label. You bring the equipment unboxed, tell them it is an AT&T return, and give them your nine-digit account number. A store representative scans the equipment, packs and ships it for you, and hands you a tracking receipt. [AT&T's return page](https://www.att.com/support/how-to/internet/equipment-return/) is the reference for all of this, and if you are weighing drop-off options, we compare [where to return AT&T equipment — FedEx, UPS, or a local shipping center](/articles/where-to-return-att-equipment-fedex-or-ups/).

**Two warnings.** Do not drop the equipment in a drop box — it must be scanned by a store rep so AT&T knows it is coming, or the fee can still apply. And watch the clock: you have 21 days from your cancel or swap date. If you cannot get to an authorized location, call AT&T at 800.288.2020 first — that number starts the return and AT&T will tell you what your options are if you cannot reach an authorized location.

That is the entire procedure. Two hours of fear, five minutes of action.
