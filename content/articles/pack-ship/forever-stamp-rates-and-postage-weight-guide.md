## The Fine Print You Never Got

You keep a sheet of Forever stamps in the drawer, you slap one on an envelope, and it goes. That works until the day it does not — the card comes back with a yellow sticker, or the invitation you spent an afternoon addressing gets held at the post office for postage due. Nobody handed you the actual rulebook. The stamp covers one ounce. Your envelope weighed 1.2. The surcharge for the way it is shaped never appeared anywhere you looked. And one consolation you may not know: Forever stamps never expire, so an old stamp still covers today's first ounce. (When USPS moves the whole rate table again, we log what it means for senders — see [the 2026 USPS rate increase explained](/articles/usps-rate-increase-online-sellers/).)

What you expected was simple: one stamp, one letter, done. What you got was a pricing table with four weight tiers, a shape rule buried in a manual, and a surcharge that applies even to envelopes lighter than the stamp's own coverage.

Here is the whole system, on one page — and how to get any envelope out the door right the first time.

## The Machine Behind The Per-Ounce Guessing Game

USPS prices First-Class Mail by two things: weight and shape. The Forever stamp tracks the 1-ounce letter price, which moved from $0.78 to $0.82 when the July 12, 2026 rate change took effect. Every tier above one ounce adds a flat 29 cents. That is the entire weight mechanism.

The shape mechanism is quieter. A letter that cannot survive automated sorting equipment — anything square, vertical, lumpy, rigid, or fastened with clasps, string, or buttons — is classified as non-machinable. Those pieces get pulled out and hand-canceled by a person, and you pay a $0.49 surcharge for the privilege, even if the envelope weighs half an ounce. A square wedding invitation needs $1.31 in postage for the same distance your $0.82 bill travels.

Neither rule is hidden, exactly. Both live in hundreds of pages of the Domestic Mail Manual and in the Notice 123 price list that changes most years. You are not stupid for missing them. You are one person with eleven Christmas cards, going up against a pricing system engineered for bulk mailers with software.

## The Feeling Every Regular Recognizes

It is the envelope coming back. Or worse — it does not come back, and the person you sent it to calls three weeks later to ask where the invitation was. You drive to the post office, wait in line behind someone mailing a live bee queen and someone renewing a passport, and a clerk turns your envelope over, weighs it on the counter scale, and says "this needed a dollar thirty-one." Not a dollar ten. Not two stamps. A dollar thirty-one. You pay it, you have learned nothing transferable, and the next lumpy envelope will catch you the same way.

Multiply that by the birthday cards, the rebate envelopes, the grandparent who still mails thank-you notes, and the guesswork becomes a small recurring tax on your attention. Not a big cost. A nagging one.

## The Assumption Nobody Questions

The assumption: a stamp is a stamp, and if it falls off the roll it belongs on the envelope. So people stack two Forever stamps on a 2-ounce letter, which works — $1.64 against an actual rate of $1.11 — and silently overpay 53 cents. Other people put one stamp on a 1-ounce square envelope, which fails outright, and never find out why theirs was the one returned.

If your envelope is rectangular, flat, flexible, and under an ounce, one Forever stamp is the cheapest way to send it. Anything else — heavier letters, odd shapes, padded mailers — ask first, because the stamp fails silently there.

## The Bill That Never Shows Up

Nobody audits the 53 cents lost to a doubled-up stamp. But the costs of guessing compound:

- **Money.** Over-stamping is the quiet leak — two stamps where the rate was $1.11, three where $1.40 sufficed. Across a year of cards and invoices, that is a drawer full of stamps you didn't need to use.
- **Time and trust.** A returned envelope costs a round trip and, worse, the thing you were mailing arrives late. Invitations, payments, deadlines — some of them do not wait politely.
- **Confidence.** The worst output of the guessing system is the hesitation. You stand there holding an envelope, unsure, and either overpay to be safe or drop it in the box and hope. Hope is not a postage strategy.

You don't need the manual. You need a scale and someone who reads it all day.
