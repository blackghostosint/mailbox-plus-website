## You Already Know What the Belt Does

You have something that cannot be replaced — your grandmother's china, a framed print you hunted down for years, camera gear that cost more than your first car — and it has to get across the country in one piece. The counters near Willowick will happily take it. They will also hand it to a national sorting network that treats it exactly like a pallet of textbooks, and you will spend the next four days refreshing a tracking page with your stomach in a knot.

That is not how an irreplaceable thing should travel. It should be packed the way fragile things are supposed to be packed — double-boxed, cushioned, labeled — by someone who does it every day, and then it should simply arrive. Here is how that works.

## The Conveyor Belt Gamble

The thing you are up against is not a careless clerk. It is the conveyor belt itself.

Every package you hand to a carrier enters an automated sorting system that moves millions of boxes a night. Belts drop packages between chutes. Heavier boxes get stacked on lighter ones. "FRAGILE" labels are a suggestion the machines cannot read. The carriers are honest about this — FedEx's terms and UPS's packing guidance both put the responsibility on you, the sender, to pack an item well enough to survive ordinary handling, and ordinary handling is exactly the drop-and-stack reality of the belt. Insurance covers the declared dollar value. It does not cover the thing itself.

Willowick has counters that will ship your box, but none that specialize in packing it. The FedEx counter at the Walgreens on Vine Street is a retail counter inside a drugstore — convenient for dropping off an already-packed box, not a place where someone builds a proper fragile crate around your heirloom. The nearest UPS Store is in Willoughby, and so is the FedEx Office — both fine stores, both about three miles away, and neither one changes the math: the box still ends up on the same belt. The only variable you actually control is how well it is packed before it gets there.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You wrap the vase in the dish towels. You wedge it in a box with last year's air pillows. You hand it over and hear the clerk ask the questions that matter — "what's the value?" — but not the question that matters more: "how is this packed?"

The clerk takes it. Somewhere tonight it rides a belt in a facility you will never see. You check the tracking that night. You check it again the next morning. Every notification ping is a small jolt until the word "Delivered" finally shows up — and even then, you watch the recipient open it on video, or you wait for the text, because insurance reimburses the receipt, not the memory.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

You would not hand a family photo album to a stranger and say "toss it in the truck." Valuable things deserve preparation, not hope. The entire burden of a fragile shipment rests on one decision — how it is packed — and that decision is made by whoever packs the box. If the answer is "you, at your kitchen table, with whatever was in the closet," you have gambled the one variable you controlled.

Packing fragile items properly is a skill. It has materials, methods, and a sequence. It should be available the same way the label is: walk in, hand it over, done right.
