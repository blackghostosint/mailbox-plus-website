## The Fine Print You Never Got

You walked into a shipping store, handed over a box, and saw a sign with a carrier's name on it. What you probably never saw is the contract behind the counter — the agreement that decides what that store can call itself, which carriers it carries, and who actually sets the price you just paid.

You should be able to look at a storefront and know exactly what you're walking into: whose network your box rides, whose rules apply, and whether the person behind the counter can offer you a second carrier when the first one prices badly.

Here's how the labels work, what each one commits a store to, and how to use that information the next time you ship.

## The Machine Behind Franchise Lock-Ins

The shipping industry runs on a patchwork of store types, and the carriers name each one differently. Once you know the labels, you can read any storefront like a spec sheet.

**UPS Authorized Shipping Outlet.** That is UPS's term for a staffed, full-service shipping operation inside an independent business. Look at any of UPS's own location pages and the pattern repeats: the listing names a host business — a mailing center, a pack-and-ship shop — and describes a location where customers can create a new shipment, drop off pre-packaged pre-labeled UPS shipments, and buy packaging supplies. The store is not a UPS building. It is someone else's counter with a UPS contract attached.

**FedEx Authorized ShipCenter.** FedEx runs the same arrangement under its own name. Its own documentation puts the number at more than 5,800 independently owned and operated pack-and-ship locations across the U.S. participating in the FedEx Authorized ShipCenter program. Most carry both FedEx Express and FedEx Ground, and most also offer faxing, copying, and other business services. FedEx also says something customers rarely notice: these locations reflect each location's own pricing policies and may apply additional charges to FedEx published rates.

**Corporate and franchise chains.** Separate from both programs are the brand chains — FedEx Office on the corporate side, The UPS Store on the franchise side, a network of independently owned stores operating under one license and one brand.

Now the mechanism that matters. A store that commits to one carrier's brand — through a franchise license or a single-carrier arrangement — sells that carrier. That is the deal. The franchisee's signage, software, and pricing all point one direction. When your package would price better on a different network, the store cannot show you that option, because the arrangement it signed doesn't include one.

The label on the door is really a statement of what the counter is allowed to tell you.

FedEx says it plainly in its own location documentation — Authorized ShipCenter locations reflect each location's own pricing policies and may apply additional charges to FedEx published rates. That means two stores can carry the same carrier, ship to the same address, and hand you two different totals for the same box. Both are legitimate. Both are operating under the terms each signed.

The customer rarely learns this before paying. The price reads like the carrier's price, because the carrier's name is on the wall. It isn't. It's the store's price, set by a business you've never heard of, under a contract you can't read.

A shipping decision made without that fact isn't really a decision. It's a guess with a receipt attached.
