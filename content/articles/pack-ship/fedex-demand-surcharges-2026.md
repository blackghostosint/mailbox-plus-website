## The Scene That Starts Everything

It is the second week of December and you are standing at the counter of your own business — if you can call the corner of the dining room a business. You run a small online shop out of your house in Concord Township. Candles. Or maybe printed shirts. Or the maple products your family has been making since before you were born. It does not matter which. What matters is that you sell the kind of thing that never quite fits a standard box — and you priced your holiday orders in September, on the shipping costs you knew.

You have shipped this exact box a hundred times. It weighs over fifty pounds, or it runs over four feet long, or it is shaped oddly enough that FedEx already charges you its standard handling fee. You know what that box costs.

You print the label for customer order 214 and stop. Under the base rate there is a line you have never seen before:

**Demand — Additional Handling Surcharge: $11.85.**

On top of the fuel percentage. On top of the standard handling fee your box already pays. Same box. Same weight. Same destination. The only thing that changed is the week.

Nobody emailed you. Nobody raised your rates. The boxes are identical. What changed is the calendar — and a pricing mechanism the carriers have spent years perfecting and almost no time explaining.

If you sell online, ship holiday gifts to family, or run any business that puts boxes on trucks, this article is about that line item. It is about what FedEx demand surcharges actually are, when they hit, and — most importantly — what you can do about them before December does.

## The Surcharge Stack

FedEx demand surcharges were once reserved for the busiest weeks of the year. Today, as [Refund Retriever's analysis](https://www.refundretriever.com/blog/fedex-peak-surcharges) puts it, they have become a regular part of the carrier's pricing strategy. FedEx itself points to elevated shipping volumes, high demand for capacity, and rising operating costs — and it expects strong residential shipping volume to continue into 2027. The translation is simple: the fee is not going away, and it is not a mistake. It is a pricing decision made by people who know exactly when you need them.

Here is the 2026-2027 schedule, straight from FedEx's published rate tables:

| Surcharge                          | Sept 28 – Nov 22 | Nov 23 – Dec 27 | Dec 28 – Jan 17 |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| Additional Handling                | $8.80            | **$11.85**      | $8.80           |
| Oversize                           | $95.75           | **$117.25**     | $95.75          |
| Ground Unauthorized Package        | $535             | **$595**        | $535            |
| Express overnight services         | $1.30            | **$2.55**       | $1.30           |
| FedEx 2Day services                | $1.20            | **$2.35**       | $1.20           |
| Ground residential & Home Delivery | $0.50            | **$0.80**       | $0.50           |
| Ground Economy (contract)          | $2.55            | **$4.05**       | $2.55           |

Three windows. The early one starts September 28. The most expensive one runs November 23 through December 27 — the exact weeks you actually need to ship. The late one tags you through January 17, right when you are sending the last stragglers and the returns.

One date worth knowing before you compare tables: the two columns above do not all start on the same day. The handling, oversize, and unauthorized-package fees begin **September 28**. The per-package demand surcharges on Express, 2Day, residential, and Ground Economy services do not kick in until **October 26**. Either way, the peak-of-peak rate — the middle column — is where the damage lands.

Now here is the part that hurts. The demand fee is charged **on top of** the standard surcharge, not instead of it. If your box already trips FedEx's ordinary Additional Handling fee — heavy, long, or oddly shaped — you pay the regular fee and the demand fee. Same for oversize. And the weekly fuel surcharge is still riding on top of all of it. That is why the same box can cost dramatically more in December without a single thing about it changing.

For the record, an oversize package is one that exceeds 96 inches in length, or 130 inches in length plus girth — the way FedEx measures it, length plus twice the height plus twice the width. One guitar, one lamp, one set of golf clubs, and you are in the oversize club, paying a fee that has nearly doubled in five years.

## What It Actually Feels Like

It feels like being charged a toll on a road you already paid for.

You stand there with the label in your hand and the number on the screen, and you have no leverage. There is nothing to negotiate, nothing to shop around for at the store you are standing in — because a single-carrier store only sells one carrier's surcharges. If you ask why it costs more than it did in September, the answer is a shrug and the words "peak season," as though the calendar itself is a natural disaster.

For online sellers it is worse. You did not price your candles in September assuming a $11.85 surprise on every box that weighs over fifty pounds or runs over four feet. Your margins were already thin. The surcharge is not a rounding error — it is a business expense that appears exactly when you can least afford it, and you are the one absorbing it, because raising prices in December makes you uncompetitive in the one month you cannot afford to be uncompetitive.

And the fee is never itemized in a way you can act on. It shows up as part of the total, in the fine print of an invoice, with no explanation and no warning.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

A surcharge is a price increase that does not look like one. Base rates went up in January. The fuel percentage climbs weekly on top of that. And now the calendar adds more on top of the fuel. At no point in that chain does anyone sit down with you and show you the whole stack. At no point does anyone own the number.

A hundred-and-seventeen-dollar fee on a box you can carry with one arm should be a headline, not a footnote. A shipping price should mean something — you should be able to promise a customer a delivery date and a cost, and keep both.

You should not need a degree in logistics to ship a holiday gift. That is not a niche skill. That is the entire job of the shipping industry, and somewhere along the way the industry started charging extra for the privilege of understanding it.
