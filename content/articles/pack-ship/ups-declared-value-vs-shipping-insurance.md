## The Burden-of-Proof Switch: How It Got This Way

Start with the number everybody runs into. Under UPS's terms and conditions of service, if you declare no value — or declare more but don't pay the charge — the carrier's maximum liability for loss or damage is $100 per package. That is the default on every UPS shipment in America: your box is covered at a hundred dollars whether it holds a $90 coffee maker or a $4,000 watch.

Declared value is the mechanism for raising that cap. Per UPS's published value-added pricing, there is no charge up to $100 of value. Above that, the retail charge is $0.90 for each $100 of declared value — any fraction of $100 counts as a full $100 — with a $2.70 minimum. Declaring a $500 package costs about $3.60 more than the base rate. Declaring a $2,000 package costs about $17.10. The ceiling is $50,000 per package on domestic services, with one brutal exception: packages containing jewelry are capped at $500, period.

Now the part that surprises people. Declared value is not technically insurance — it's a change to the carrier's liability. But read the ground-transportation terms closely and the wall between the two blurs: UPS states that excess valuation charges are remitted to an insurance company as shipper's interest insurance, and that claims are filed with and settled by UPS on behalf of that insurer. So the money you pay for "declared value" ends up as an insurance premium anyway. The label changes; the claim process does not.

And here is the switch that flips without warning. The moment a claim exists, the burden of proof sits on you, the shipper. UPS's own claims documentation says every claim requires supporting documentation — an invoice to establish value, photos during a damage claim, and possibly a physical inspection at a UPS-approved location. Firms that process carrier claims for a living report the same denial reason over and over, year after year: insufficient packaging. If the carrier's investigator decides the box didn't meet its packing guidelines, the claim can be denied even when the declared-value fee was paid in full.

So the same transaction that looks like protection at the counter contains a second, invisible contract: pack to the standard or the coverage you paid for can evaporate. Nobody hands you that contract. You find out about it while reading a denial email.

## Why This Quietly Stopped Making Sense

None of this means declared value is a scam. A $4,000 instrument travels at $100 of protection by default, and that gap is real — declared value exists to close it, and the price is reasonable. USPS plays a parallel game: Ground Advantage, Priority Mail, and Priority Mail Express each include up to $100 of insurance in the postage price, with additional coverage purchasable up to $5,000.

The problem is the gap between what customers think they're buying and what the terms deliver. You think you bought an outcome — "if it breaks, I get my money." What you actually bought is a seat at a claims process where the outcome depends on evidence you may not have thought to keep: the original invoice, photos before unpacking, the box, the cushioning, every scrap of it, retained until the claim concludes. Claims-help firms report the same failure over and over: all packaging material must be kept until the claim is resolved, and a package whose evidence was thrown out can get the claim denied.

A protection product whose protection depends on homework the buyer was never told to do isn't protection. It's a bet the buyer didn't know they placed. That's what should bother you — not the fee, but the fine print doing the deciding.

## What Nobody Tells You You're Paying

**In lost claims.** Every denial that turns on packaging is a full loss of the item's value over the $100 default — often hundreds or thousands of dollars, gone over a carton choice that took seconds. That is the most expensive packing shortcut in retail.

**In discarded evidence.** The receiver who sensibly throws away a crushed box and its foam peanuts has just deleted the claim's central evidence. Carriers can require the item and packaging for inspection; if they're gone, the claim usually dies with them. Nobody warns the person holding the box.

**In missed windows.** UPS's terms deem claims waived if not made within nine months of delivery — but practically, damage claims run on much tighter rhythms, since photos and inspection need to happen while the evidence is fresh. Waiting weeks to "see if it works out" is a decision, and it's usually the wrong one.

**In the quiet tax of dreading it.** You refresh the tracking page at lunch, then again before bed, doing the math on what the $100 default would actually cover if the screen says delivered but the box arrives crushed. That low-grade mental recalculating follows every shipment of anything valuable — and it never prints an invoice.
