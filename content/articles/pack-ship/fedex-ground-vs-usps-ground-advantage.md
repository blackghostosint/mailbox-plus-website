You have a package. You have an address. You have a vague memory that one of the ground services is cheaper than the other, and no idea which one. ## Say It Plainly: The Counter Friction

Let me lay out the real differences, plainly, using only what the carriers themselves publish.

**USPS Ground Advantage** is the Postal Service's ground product. Retail prices start at $8.40 at a Post Office location. It's marketed with a typical delivery window of 2-5 days — a window, not a promise. It accepts packages up to 70 lbs with a maximum combined length and girth of 130 inches. It includes $100 of insurance per shipment, outbound and return, and you can buy additional coverage against loss or damage for merchandise up to $5,000. Packages past the ounce-tier pricing boundary get priced at pound rates. And there are nonstandard fees that can sneak up on you: $4.50 for packages in the longer-length nonstandard tier, $21.00 for packages over 2 cubic feet, and $4.00 for nonstandard shapes or materials.

**FedEx Ground** (and Home Delivery for residences) takes bigger, heavier freight: up to 150 lbs, up to 108 inches long, and up to 165 inches combined length plus girth. That's the headline difference. If your package is over the USPS limits, FedEx Ground is usually the ground service that can still take it.

And the one that trips people up constantly: **PO Boxes.** Only USPS may deliver to a PO Box. UPS cannot, and FedEx Ground generally cannot. If your recipient only has a PO Box — no street address — USPS Ground Advantage is the ground option that completes the delivery instead of bouncing the package back to you.

That's the whole scoreboard. Everything else — which one is cheaper on Tuesday for a 4-pound box going to Columbus — depends on the live rate, which changes.

## Simple Things, Made Complicated on Purpose

There's a reason the comparison feels deliberately murky: each carrier publishes its own pricing structure, its own fee schedule, its own dimensional weight rules, and its own delivery-window language. None of them are designed to be compared. They're designed to be _chosen_ — ideally by you committing to one carrier for everything and never looking sideways again.

So the internet fills the gap with comparison articles, and most of them are worse than useless, because they quote rates that were live at some point and aren't anymore. Shipping rates are volatile. A price table in an article is a photograph of last season. The live rate at the counter is the only one that's real.

This is also why the "which is best" framing is the wrong question. Best for whom? A box of books going to a street address in another state? Often one answer. A 2-pound padded mailer going to a PO Box? USPS, almost by default, because of the PO Box rule. An extra-heavy piece of equipment? FedEx Ground, because USPS Ground Advantage stops at 70 lbs. The service follows the package, not the other way around.

## The Cost of Leaving It Alone

Here's what the guess-and-hope approach actually costs people.

**Wrong carrier, wrong box.** You pick USPS Ground Advantage for something that turns out to be over 70 lbs or shaped in a way that triggers nonstandard fees — the $4.50, the $21.00, the rest of the stack — and your "cheap" rate can climb fast.

**The PO Box bounce.** You send via FedEx Ground to an address that's actually a PO Box. It doesn't complete the delivery. Now you've paid for shipping twice and your recipient is annoyed. Only USPS delivers to PO Boxes — that's not a preference, it's the rule.

**The underinsured loss.** USPS Ground Advantage includes $100 of insurance per shipment. If your item is worth more than that and you didn't add coverage — or you shipped via a service with different declared-value rules and assumed they matched — a loss becomes your loss.

**The time.** This is the one nobody prices. The tabs, the charts, the second-guessing. Shipping shouldn't cost you an hour. It costs you an hour anyway when you insist on doing the carrier's comparison shopping for it.
