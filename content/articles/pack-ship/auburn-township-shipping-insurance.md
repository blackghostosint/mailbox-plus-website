## The Hundred-Dollar Cliff

The mechanism has a name in the industry: released value. It's printed in every carrier's terms of service, and every carrier's version is the same shape.

UPS's tariff states its liability for loss or damage to each domestic package is limited to $100 unless you declare a greater value and pay the charge. FedEx's page says the same thing from the other direction: the first $100 of value is included in your shipping rate as part of their standard $100 limit of liability. USPS builds up to $100 of insurance into Priority Mail, Priority Mail Express, and Ground Advantage — but that's the ceiling on those services, and coverage stops at that hundred unless you buy more. Even the pack-and-ship guarantee at a franchise store drops to $100 if the item's value was never declared at the transaction.

Here's the part almost nobody knows, because the counter never explains it: declaring a value is not buying insurance. UPS's own terms say a shipper who declares a value over $100 "does not receive any form of insurance." It's a liability ceiling adjustment, and it comes with rules — proof of value, proof the carrier was at fault, and proof the packaging met their standard. FedEx says outright that a package damaged in transit because it was improperly packed earns nothing, declared value or not.

So the default protects the first hundred dollars, and the upgrade protects nothing if the box gives the carrier an out. That is the system working exactly as written.

## What It Actually Feels Like

The tracking page says delivered. The buyer's photos say destroyed. Or the tracking page says nothing at all, day after day, and the word "lost" starts replacing "delayed" in your head.

You dig out the receipt. You fill out the claim form. And then you meet the questions that decide everything: What was inside? Prove it — a listing, an appraisal, a photo from before it shipped. Was it packed properly? Prove that too. Somewhere in the form is the sentence that ends it: reimbursement is limited to the declared value, and claims may be denied when packaging doesn't meet the standard.

You declared nothing, because nobody told you declaring was a thing. The box was packed on your kitchen table with newspaper and tape, because that's how everybody packs. The claim comes back for $100 — or comes back with a denial. The antique is gone either way, and the carrier keeps pointing at the terms you never read.

The sting isn't just the money. It's finding out the promise you thought you were paying for was never on the receipt.
