## The Scene That Starts Everything

It is 2:14 PM on a Wednesday in Concord Township. You get a notification on your phone: "Your Amazon package has been delivered."

You are at work. You will not be home for another four hours. The package — a birthday gift for your niece — is sitting on your front porch. Right next to the street. Visible to anyone walking by.

You tell yourself it will be fine. This is a quiet neighborhood. Nothing ever happens here.

At 6:47 PM, you pull into your driveway. The porch is empty. The package is gone. No box. No wrapping. Nothing.

You check with your neighbors. Nobody saw anything. You check your Ring doorbell footage. A person in a hooded sweatshirt walked up your driveway at 2:22 PM, picked up the box, and walked off. Eight minutes after the delivery driver dropped it off.

The package was on your porch for less than ten minutes. That was all it took.

## Porch Pirates

This is not a one-time event. It is a systematic problem that follows a predictable pattern. Porch pirates — the informal name for package thieves who follow delivery trucks — operate in every neighborhood in Lake County. They look for houses where packages sit unattended. They know the delivery schedules of Amazon, UPS, and FedEx. They know which neighborhoods have the most daytime deliveries.

Here is how they work. A driver drops off a package. The thief, who may have been waiting in a parked car down the street, walks up to the porch, picks up the box, and leaves. The whole interaction takes less than fifteen seconds. They are not breaking into your house. They are not stealing your car. They are taking what you left in plain sight, and in Ohio, that is a low-risk, high-reward crime.

The numbers are sobering. According to security camera data and insurance claims, roughly one in five homeowners has had a package stolen at some point. In higher-density areas like Mentor, Painesville, and Eastlake, the rate is closer to one in four.

The thieves count on the fact that you will not be home. And most of the time, they are right.

## What It Actually Feels Like

It is not the cost of the package that stings the most — although that hurts. It is the violation. Somebody walked onto your property, onto your porch, and took something that belonged to you. While you were at work. While your kids were at school. While you trusted that your neighborhood was safe.

You start checking your security camera compulsively. You text your spouse when a delivery notification comes through: "Did you get the package?" You ask a neighbor to grab it if they see it sitting there. You change your Amazon delivery instructions to "Hide behind the pillar" or "Leave by the garage."

You adapt your life around the fear of theft. You schedule deliveries for weekends. You ship to your office. You drive to the carrier facility after work to pick up packages that should have been left on your porch.

The low-level anxiety becomes background noise. Every notification is a potential loss. Every delivery is a gamble.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

The expectation when you order something is simple: you pay for it, and it arrives at your door. You should not have to build a security system around your front porch to protect a twenty-dollar shipment from Amazon.

But the delivery system is designed for the carrier's convenience, not yours. The driver drops the package, takes a photo as proof, and moves on. Whether it is still there an hour later is not their problem. The tracking status says "Delivered." The carrier's obligation is fulfilled.

That leaves you holding the risk. You paid for the item. You chose the delivery address. If the package disappears between the driver's photo and your arrival home, you are the one filing the claim, calling the retailer, waiting for the replacement.

The system is broken. The driver is incentivized to complete the delivery, not to ensure the package reaches your hands. And the thief is incentivized to grab it before you do.
