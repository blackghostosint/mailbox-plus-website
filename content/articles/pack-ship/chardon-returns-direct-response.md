# Three Returns. Three Stores. One Saturday.

![Three packages on a counter with different carrier labels](articles/pack-ship/chardon-returns-direct-response-featured.webp)

## The Back Seat Full of Boxes

You have three returns in the back seat of your car.

The first one is a sweater from Nordstrom. It arrived in a UPS box. The return label on the Nordstrom website printed a UPS barcode. You printed it last night, folded the sweater back into the original box, and taped it shut with packing tape you found in the kitchen drawer. The tape is not the right kind for shipping. You know this. You hope they do not notice.

The second box is a pair of running shoes from Zappos. Zappos uses FedEx. Always has. The shoe box is lighter than the Nordstrom box. You tossed the original packaging by accident, so you put the shoes in a priority mail box your neighbor gave you. You know you are not supposed to use USPS boxes for FedEx. But it is what you had.

The third is a leather wallet from an Etsy shop in Portland. The seller used USPS Priority Mail. You are supposed to drop this one at any post office counter.

Three items. Three carriers. Three different stores.

There are no UPS Stores in Chardon. There are no FedEx stores in Chardon. The post office is at 150 Center Street, but it only handles USPS.

If you want to return all three, you are driving to Mentor.

## The Return Runaround

The Return Runaround is not a policy written anywhere. No company sat in a conference room and decided to make returning things difficult. It emerged organically from the way the shipping industry works.

UPS built its own stores. FedEx built its own stores. The post office handles only USPS. None of them have any reason to take the other guy's boxes. Every package you drop at the UPS Store is revenue for UPS. Every package at the post office is revenue for USPS. They are not in the business of helping their competitors.

So the system works like this: you buy from three different retailers, each one has a contract with a different carrier, and each carrier requires you to return the package through their own network. There is no universal return counter. There is no place that says "give me all of them, I will handle it."

You are the universal return counter. Your car is the sorting facility. Your Saturday is the operating window.

## What It Feels Like on Saturday Morning

You leave the house at 9:30. The plan is simple: drop three packages, grab coffee, be home by 10:30. The reality looks different.

First stop is the UPS Store at 9401 Mentor Avenue. You drive 20 minutes from Chardon, past the square, down Route 44, over to Mentor Avenue. You park, grab the Nordstrom box, walk in. The line is four people deep. One person is shipping an entire household — boxes stacked on a dolly, labels being printed one at a time, the counter person moving in slow motion. You wait twelve minutes. The sweater gets scanned. You get a receipt. You walk out.

It is 9:52. You are already behind schedule.

Second stop is the FedEx Office at 8390 Mentor Avenue — about five minutes further east on the same road. You pull into the lot, grab the shoe box in the wrong packaging, walk in. The line here is shorter, but the person at the counter has to look up the drop-off procedure. They raise an eyebrow at the USPS box but take it. You get a receipt.

It is 10:05.

Now you have a decision to make. The USPS package needs a post office. You can drive back to Chardon — the post office at 150 Center Street is open until 1:00 on Saturday. Or you can drive north to the Painesville post office at 215 East Jackson Street. Either way, it is another 15 to 20 minutes of driving.

You choose Chardon. You drive back. You drop the wallet in the outgoing mail slot. It is 10:25.

You have been running errands for almost an hour. You still do not have coffee. You still have not started the things you actually wanted to do today.

This is the Return Runaround. It is not a crisis. It is not an emergency. It is just a quiet, persistent drain on your time that you do not notice until you look at the clock and realize your morning is gone.

You ordered three things online. You did not order a Saturday of logistics.

The retailers made the choice about which carrier to use. You did not get a vote. One chose UPS. One chose FedEx. One chose USPS. And all three expected you to figure out how to get your money back by running a three-stop route from Chardon to Mentor and back again.

There is a better way. It does not require the shipping industry to change. It just requires one counter that refuses to play the carrier loyalty game.

## What Keeps Happening If You Do Not Switch

The numbers are small per trip. Twelve minutes here. Nine minutes there. A second trip to a second store.

But they add up.

If you return items three times a month — which is not unusual for an average online shopper — you are spending roughly an hour and a half per month running the Return Runaround. That is eighteen hours a year. Eighteen hours of driving, waiting, and standing in lines.

Eighteen hours is a vacation day. It is three full Saturdays. It is the time you could spend on things that actually matter.

The frustration compounds too. Every time you stand in a line wondering why the nearest UPS Store and FedEx Office are both in Mentor instead of in Chardon, the annoyance gets a little heavier. It is not the end of the world. But it is a weight you carry every time you order something online and remember what happens when it does not work out.

You can keep carrying that weight. Or you can make one change and stop.
