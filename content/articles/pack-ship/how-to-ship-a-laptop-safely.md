Your laptop has to reach somebody, and the box it came in went to the recycling bin ages ago. That is the whole problem. A laptop is a screen, a drive, and a lithium-ion battery riding through a network of trucks, belts, and sorters built for speed, and whether it arrives working or arrives cracked comes down almost entirely to how it was packed. The outcome you want is uneventful: it powers on at the destination, no claim gets filed, and you did not surrender an afternoon getting there. The how is just as plain. ## The Rule That Only Helps One Side

Here is the part of shipping a laptop that quietly costs people money. Carriers include a baseline of declared-value coverage — $100 — and for a machine worth many times that, the baseline is a rounding error. UPS itself suggests considering an increased declared value for laptop shipments to guard against damage or loss, which tells you the carriers know the baseline is thin. At our counter, whenever the $100 default comes up, we also walk through Marsh third-party declared-value coverage of up to $50,000 per package, so the gap between what your laptop is worth and what a claim would actually pay can be closed. There is precedent for treating high-value shipments differently, too: FedEx rules say jewelry valued over $500 cannot travel in a padded envelope and must go in a corrugated box. A laptop deserves at least that much respect.

The lithium rules tilt the same way. Heat and static electricity associated with air travel are risk factors for the lithium-ion batteries inside laptops, so the machine must be unable to switch itself on in transit, and any required lithium-battery labels or notices go on the outside of the box. FedEx guidance caps cells at a 20 Wh rating and batteries at 100 Wh, and it bars defective or bulging batteries outright — those can catch fire and do not ship, period. USPS adds its own wrinkle: lithium batteries installed in small electronics like laptops are usually fine to mail when the item is new or a manufacturer-certified repair, but used lithium-battery items can travel only by ground and cannot go on planes. And if a package contains three or more electronic devices, the "Nature and Quantity of Goods" section of the air waybill must be completed, with air shipments of lithium ion batteries noted as "lithium ion batteries in compliance with Section two of PI 967."

## A Short Visit at the Counter

The heading is about your part of the visit, and your part is short because the process has three steps and the counter does the skilled labor in all of them.

You bring the laptop in — unboxed, untaped, whatever state it is in. The staff handles the packing the way UPS recommends for laptops: confirm the machine is completely powered down, with the battery left installed, because removing it is explicitly something you should not attempt. The power and volume buttons get a small patch of cardboard or electrical tape so the machine cannot switch on in transit, and thin bubble wrap or foam goes between the keyboard and the screen. The laptop goes into a snug-fitting plastic bag to guard against static, accessories get wrapped separately in bubble wrap secured with cable ties, and the remaining space gets foam inserts rather than loose fill. For double boxing, the outer box should be about six inches wider in all dimensions than the internal container, with the gap filled with inflatable packaging or foam — because the original manufacturer box, tidy as it looks, was designed for palletized shipping, which is exactly why UPS recommends double boxing. Then the box gets a gentle shake to confirm nothing slides inside, and it gets sealed tightly to keep moisture out.

You pick the carrier and the speed within the rules that apply, the label gets printed, and you leave with a tracking receipt. That is the whole visit: bring it, watch it get packed, choose the compliant route. Everything the counter handles is laid out at /pack-ship/.

## Run the Numbers on Standing Still

The DIY route is not free; it is just unbilled. Price out the box, the foam, the tape, the anti-static bag, and the gas — then price out the do-over if the packing fails and the claim lands against a $100 baseline, unless you have closed the gap with Marsh third-party declared-value coverage of up to $50,000 per package. Now look at the shipping options themselves, because they are not interchangeable for a laptop. USPS caps a domestic package at 70 lbs, which a laptop is nowhere near, but the service tiers matter: Priority Mail Express runs 1–3 days, Priority Mail runs 2–3 business days, and Ground Advantage runs 2–5 business days. That last tier matters more than people realize, because a used lithium-battery laptop cannot fly under USPS rules — ground is not the cheap option, it is the compliant option.
