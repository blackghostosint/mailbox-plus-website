## The Part Nobody Warns You About

You have a heavy box to ship, and the price you were quoted does not match the price you were expecting. Maybe it was the online estimate that looked fine until you entered the dimensions. Maybe it was the counter where a 30-pound box somehow cost more than the 70-pound box you shipped last month. Either way, you are standing there wondering how the weight you know went into the box turned into a number that big.

It should be simple: heavier box, higher price, done. And for most ground shipments, once you know which carrier owns which weight range — and which traps stack fees on top of the base rate — it can be. Here's how the three ground options actually price a heavy box, and where the extra money hides.

## The Machine Behind The Blanket Rate Card

The thing working against you is not one carrier being greedy. It is the way all three carriers price ground service: by published rate tables that charge by weight tier and distance zone, then pile adjustments on top. The structure is public and legal — you just never see it unless you go looking.

Start with weight tiers. Every carrier breaks ground pricing into pound bands, and the price does not rise smoothly between them. A 32-pound box can sit in the same tier as a 35-pound box, or jump to the next one — and two pounds of extra packing material can bump you across a line that costs real money. USPS Ground Advantage tops out at 70 pounds; past that, you are out of options there. UPS Ground and FedEx Ground both accept up to 150 pounds, 108 inches in length, or 165 inches in length plus girth.

Then comes dimensional weight. If your box is large and light — a lamp base, a bundle of pool noodles, foam-packed anything — the carriers bill it by the space it occupies, not the number on the scale. You pay for air. A box that weighs 12 pounds can be billed as 30 if it measures big enough.

And on top of the base rate sit the surcharges. Fuel surcharges on domestic ground ran around 29.75% at both UPS and FedEx in the fall of 2026 — set weekly, applied to nearly every package. Cross 50 pounds or certain size thresholds and additional-handling fees start — we unpack the full surcharge stack in [what happens when your box is heavy or oversized](/articles/heavy-oversized-package-shipping-freight-alternative/). During the holiday peak, UPS raised its additional-handling fee to $11.90 per package and the large-package fee to $117.50 between November 22 and December 26. None of that shows up in the weight you're thinking about.

## The Moment Your Hand Stops on Your Wallet

You packed the box carefully. You knew the weight — you were the one who lugged it to the car. Then the total comes back, and it's higher than the online quote. Your hand stops on your wallet. Nobody up front explains why; the rules that produced the number were never shown to you.

You start second-guessing yourself. Should you have used a smaller box? Should you have split it into two shipments? Should you drive somewhere else? Most people just pay it, because the alternative — auditing three carriers' rate tables on your phone in a parking lot — is not a real option when you have somewhere to be.

## What the Counter Knows and Can't Say

USPS Ground Advantage is often the lowest sticker price for mid-weight boxes up to 70 pounds. Retail starts at $7.90, typical delivery runs 2 to 5 days. Its weakness: at 70 pounds you hit a hard ceiling, and pricing climbs quickly in the upper weight tiers — a heavy box near the cap can cost more than the same box at UPS or FedEx, which price their upper tiers for heavier freight.

**UPS Ground and FedEx Ground** run nearly identical rulebooks: 150 pounds, 108-inch length, 165-inch length-plus-girth. Their per-pound pricing is built for heavier boxes, so as your shipment climbs from 40 toward 70-plus pounds, they often pull ahead of USPS on price. Their weakness is the surcharge stack — that ~30% fuel charge, additional handling past 50 pounds, and residential delivery fees.

So we don't pick by logo. We put your actual box on the scale, measure it, and rate your actual destination — then show you the lowest total. Anyone who tells you one carrier always wins on heavy boxes, or that a single weight cutoff flips the winner forever, is reciting a rule of thumb that breaks the moment a real box crosses a real tier line.
