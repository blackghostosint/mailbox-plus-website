## The Notice That Never Reached Your Counter

Indianapolis. Last scan 4:12 p.m. Nothing since. The tracking page still shows the facility name, and none of the honesty. On September 21, the Postal Service posted a warning about exactly this: packages moving through its Indianapolis and Louisville regional processing hubs may run late for the next few weeks. If your label routes through either of those buildings, that notice is about your box — and it was not written to you.

You did not cause this and you could not have prevented it. The cause is a FAST posting — the industry board, not your inbox — announcing new sorting machines going in at Indianapolis while Louisville eats the volume of Evansville, Indiana, which is closed for its own upgrade. USPS said so openly: customers of those facilities may experience shipping delays over the next few weeks, right as the peak holiday surge begins.

You can absorb that if you know it before you buy the label. You cannot absorb it if you find out on day four, holding your phone at 11:40 at night, refreshing a page that has not changed since Thursday.

## The Industry Facility Notice

FAST is the message board trade desks read. Your inbox is not on it. That is why the delay arrives looking like your fault. The Indianapolis work: machines that can sort large, oversized packages that used to be handled by hand, finalized over the next two to three weeks, with routing changes "to improve operational flows and cycle times into and out of the region." Louisville, meanwhile, has been fighting delays through all of September because it is absorbing the load of the Evansville, Indiana, processing site, which is closed for its own upgrades. On September 22, USPS said some volume from the affected facilities will divert to Nashville, Tennessee, and to an annex facility in Springdale, Ohio.

None of that posting mentioned your name, your buyer, or your promise date. So the delay lands as your failure — the buyer texts you, the review names you, the refund comes out of your margin — while the routing change sits three states upstream, doing exactly what it was designed to do.

They say transit times will improve "once these installations are completed and operations stabilize." Your buyer did not order "once the machines settle." A routing change approved in September does not know your package shipped on the 26th.

## The Part That Wears You Down

The phone is face-up on the kitchen table, screen still lit, because you have checked it nine times since dinner and nine times it has said the same thing. The scan still reads the same Indiana facility it read at 4:12 that afternoon. Across the room, the tape gun is still sitting on the counter where you left it, next to the scraps of kraft paper from the box you thought was handled. The house is quiet enough to hear the refrigerator hum over the fan of the laptop you keep open to the same tab.

You open the buyer's message again. Three words: "Where is this??" Sent at 9:07. You type "It's in transit" and delete it, because you have no idea where it is and you will not send a sentence you would not believe. The tracking page now says "In Transit, Arriving Late" — a machine admitting your package exists but refusing to say where. And there is the second screen, the listing, where a buyer for whom "took forever to ship" is a sentence already being drafted. You did everything right. The network did not. The review will never know the difference.

## The Store That Takes the Headache

We read those facility notices anyway — a shipping counter is one of the few places where that homework has a job. The laminated carrier rate sheets by the register get swapped when the surcharge tables move, and the facility notices get read the same week they post, because a package that stalls is the only receipt anyone remembers. So when your box comes across our counter and the destination ZIP routes through Indianapolis, Louisville, Evansville, or the diverted lanes, we say it before you pay: "That lane's running through Louisville this week — here's the carrier that isn't." You see both numbers, the clean lane and the dirty one, and you choose. The full comparison — how this works package by package, all year — is the same logic behind [comparing shipping rates before you buy](/articles/compare-shipping-rates-ups-fedex-usps-dhl/).

## How to Ship Around It

You set the item on the counter — no tape, no label, no carrier decision, no apology needed — and you say where it goes and the day it truly has to be there. We weigh it, quote every carrier on the same counter, and flag the dirty lanes: "That ZIP is sitting in the Louisville rebuild. UPS leaves from this same counter tonight and does not. Here's both numbers."

If your deadline is real, we do not hand you a service tier and a hope. During an upgrade window, "2-day" is a tier, not a promise — so the buffer goes into the arrival date before you pay, and you never have to guess what "2-day" means while Indianapolis is being rewired. If no carrier can honestly hit your date, you hear that at the counter, with a receipt in your hand — not from a buyer three days later.

## What You Lose by Shipping Through the Notice Blind

The reship comes out of the same margin as the review that says you were slow. The cheapest label wins the day you buy it and loses the month: the delay you did not see coming turns into the refund you reissue, the discounted replacement you extend, and the one-star that calls you slow for a mistake made inside a facility you have never seen. You did not get to vote on the routing change. But you pay for it anyway — unless somebody at the counter read the notice on Thursday and priced around it before you did. The arithmetic is the same one that decides [when a USPS rate increase actually reaches your wallet](/articles/usps-rate-increase-online-sellers/): the label price is only ever half the cost.

## Your Next Shipment, Handled

You pull into the lot off Fredle Drive and carry the box in — packed, half-packed, or just the item in your hands. The clerk reads the destination ZIP, taps twice on the screen, and says, "Saturday's real — but not on the Indianapolis lane, it's mid-rebuild. This box goes UPS tonight." While you were walking in, the tape gun on the bench was still warm from the last box; now it is yours for thirty seconds, and the label prints, and they slide the receipt across the counter with the arrival date already circled. "You can put your phone down. The dirty lane is not the one this box is on." That is what the promise sounds like when it is made by someone who read the notice on Thursday — not by a tracking page at 11:40.

## No Prep, No Apologies: Just Hand It Over
