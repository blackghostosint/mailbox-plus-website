## The $2.5 Billion Hole

On August 7, 2026, the U.S. Postal Service released its third-quarter financial results for fiscal year 2026. The headline number: a net loss of $2.5 billion for the three months ending June 30.

That is down from a $3.1 billion loss in the same quarter last year — a $562 million improvement, mainly from revenue growth in Ground Advantage shipping and Marketing Mail. The Postal Service's controllable loss narrowed to $1.0 billion from $1.6 billion.

But here is the part that matters if you live in Concord Township or anywhere else in Lake County: the improvement is not enough. Not even close.

"You are out of cash. We are borrowing from employees' retirement funds to continue operations," Postmaster General David Steiner told Congress in June. On Friday he added that without Congressional action, the agency's plans "would certainly have to entail changes that will impact service like taking a look at our service levels and closing thousands of unprofitable post offices, as well as raising prices."

Let that sink in. **Thousands** of post offices. Closed.

The Postal Service has lost more than $120 billion since 2007. First-class mail — historically its most profitable product — is in a long, slow decline as bills, statements, and correspondence move online. Meanwhile, the agency is required by law to deliver to 170 million addresses six days a week. That costs $3.4 billion a year. Seventy percent of those routes lose money. Fifty-eight percent of its 18,000 post offices lose money.

The Post Office in your town may be one of them.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

A two-minute transaction should not require navigating a federal financial crisis.

The Postal Service's problems are structural and political. It is a self-financing agency that cannot control its own pricing, its own debt limit (capped at $15 billion since 1992), or its own pension obligations. It must maintain a universal delivery network that is expensive and inefficient by design — serving every address in the country six days a week, even when 70% of those routes lose money.

These are not problems any single post office can fix. They are not problems a clerk behind a counter can solve. They are baked into the agency's DNA by an act of Congress from 1970 that has not kept pace with how people actually communicate and ship things. The Postal Service's own CFO said it plainly: "Management actions alone will not resolve ongoing financial problems that are caused by an outdated business model."

Meanwhile, the solutions being proposed — closing thousands of post offices, raising prices, cutting delivery days — all fall on you, the customer.
