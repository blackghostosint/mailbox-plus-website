## You Measured It Twice and the Website Rejected It Three Times

You have a machine part, a piece of furniture, or something else that flatly does not fit in the shipping boxes stacked next to your dryer. Somebody in another state wants it. You found a tape measure, wrote the dimensions on the back of an envelope, and typed them into a carrier's calculator — and the calculator turned hostile: surcharges you never budgeted, an error on the third try, or a flat refusal that gives you no reason and no alternative.

The carrier will not tell you this, so here it is: the number that decides everything is girth — 2× width plus 2× height, added to the length — and every carrier scores it against your money after the tape is already on the box. Single-brand counters only read you one surcharge card, so you never hear what the other carriers would charge for the same box. Here is how the math actually works, and why it keeps ambushing the people it should be helping.

## The 130-Inch Ceiling

Every carrier draws its own hard line, with a different surcharge structure behind each one.

The numbers, from the carriers' own published standards:

- **USPS** accepts domestic parcels up to **70 pounds**, and no more than **130 inches in combined length and girth** (108 inches for Priority Mail and Priority Mail Express). Anything over either limit is nonmailable — and if an oversize or overweight item gets into the postal network anyway, USPS assesses a **$200 fee** before it is released.
- **UPS** small-package service tops out at **150 pounds per package**, **108 inches in length**, and **165 inches in combined length and girth**. Cross 130 inches in length-plus-girth or 96 inches in length and you trigger a Large Package surcharge; exceed the maximums and the package is subject to an Over Maximum Limits charge.
- **FedEx Ground and Home Delivery** sit at the same ceiling: **150 pounds, 108 inches in length, 165 inches in length plus girth**.

Here is the machine inside those numbers. The formula is public — girth is 2× width plus 2× height, added to the longest side. What the carriers do not put in front of you is the timing: the correction lands after the item is accepted, taped, and on its way. A rate correction, a refused parcel, or a two-hundred-dollar federal fee can surface weeks later, and the burden of knowing the formula was on you the whole time. And at a single-brand counter, the only surcharge card anyone reads you is that brand's — you never hear that another carrier treats your exact box differently. On oversize items, the spread between carriers can be larger than the price of the box itself, and the person paying is structurally kept from seeing it.

This is all completely legal and completely ordinary. It is also why the same awkward item can cost wildly different amounts at two counters, depending entirely on which carrier's size table the clerk happened to check first — and why size and structure, not carelessness, decide most damage claims on fragile freight like glass and ceramics ([how to pack glass and ceramics so the claim holds](/articles/how-to-ship-fragile-glass-and-ceramics/)). The same system shapes the everyday drop-offs most people never think twice about ([a look inside the shop in Chardon Township](/articles/pack-ship-chardon-township/)).
