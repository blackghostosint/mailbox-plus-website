It should be simple: tell me which batteries ship, which do not, and what the box needs to look like.

## Say It Plainly: The Guessing Game

The systemic mechanism here is not any single carrier. It is the gap between how batteries are classified on paper and how that gets explained at the counter.

Batteries are not one product in the regulations. They split into four very different categories, each with its own UN number:

- **UN 3480** — lithium-ion batteries shipped alone (no equipment)
- **UN 3481** — lithium-ion batteries installed in or packed with equipment
- **UN 3090** — lithium metal batteries shipped alone
- **UN 3091** — lithium metal batteries installed in or packed with equipment

That is the entire regulatory system in four lines, and almost nobody tells you it exists. So you show up with a power-tool battery, the clerk guesses, and you get a different answer depending on who is standing there. All lithium batteries are Class 9 miscellaneous dangerous goods under federal transportation law (49 CFR). A mis-declared lithium shipment can be traced to the sender. Fines follow. So does the fire report.

Meanwhile, the humble alkaline battery sits on the other end of the scale. USPS Publication 52 says common household dry-cell batteries (AA, AAA, C, D and similar) are generally not regulated as hazardous materials and are mailable. No hazmat paperwork. No special marks. Your box of AAs was never the problem — nobody ever explained the line between the two. (The reverse trips people up too: a [USPS Priority box cannot take a FedEx or UPS label](/articles/usps-box-fedex-label/).)

## How It Feels, Honestly

You are standing at a counter holding a battery you cannot quite describe. "It's the rechargeable one from the drill." The clerk asks if it's lithium. You genuinely do not know — the label is worn off, or it says "Li-ion" in letters small enough that you're not sure it matters.

Now the clock is running. The line behind you is growing. You are doing math you were never equipped to do: is this the kind that can fly, the kind that can only ride a truck, or the kind that cannot ship at all? And the penalty for guessing wrong is not just a rejected box — it is a package that gets pulled somewhere in the middle of the country, three weeks of silence, and a phone call where you try to explain watt-hours to a stranger.

Nobody feels stupid at home. They feel stupid at the counter. That is the part that should not happen.

## It Doesn't Have to Work Like This

Here is the unfair part: the rules are public, precise, and genuinely workable. UPS publishes a battery guide that lays out terminal protection in illustrated steps. USPS Postal Explorer puts the full lithium rules online for free. FedEx publishes the UN numbers and the exact pre-approval email address for lithium battery shippers. The system is not secret.

What's broken is the last mile of that information — the moment where a shipper with one box and ten minutes needs a yes or a no. When the knowledge lives only inside hazmat training manuals, the default experience becomes trial and error with a dangerous-goods classification as the stake. A shipper who has never heard the term "UN 3481" is not careless. They were handed a technical system with no interpreter.

The distinctions that trip people up are the ones we deal with constantly:

- **Alkaline and household dry-cell** — mailable and shippable. Terminals protected, done.
- **Lithium installed in or packed with equipment** — the common case: a phone, a laptop, a drill with its charger battery in the same box. Simpler rules, a lithium battery mark when quantities exceed the exceptions.
- **Standalone lithium** — the hard case. USPS surface only. UPS and FedEx air require fully regulated dangerous-goods handling with a contract. Worth knowing before you drive anywhere.
- **Damaged, defective, or recalled lithium** — restricted or forbidden, depending on the carrier and the mode. USPS prohibits them in international mail entirely. UPS restricts them to ground service with pre-approval. This is the "swollen tablet battery" scenario, and it has its own rules for a reason.

If your shipment needs fully regulated dangerous-goods service, we will tell you that plainly — that is a carrier contract situation, not a counter situation, and pretending otherwise gets people into trouble. What we can do is identify what you have, tell you which lane it falls into, and package it to the standard the rules describe.

## The Cost of Leaving It Alone

Guess wrong about a battery and the costs stack up in three directions.

**Time.** A rejected battery shipment is not a quick retry. The box comes back or gets held, you re-research from scratch, and the item sits. Compare that to a single counter visit where the classification happens once, correctly.

**Money.** A lithium battery that starts a fire in transit is the nightmare scenario federal regulators write rules about, and enforcement lands on the shipper of record — fines and penalties, per UPS's own published warning. Even short of that, a mis-declared package pays for the trip twice.

**The thing you were actually sending.** A tablet with a cracked screen that can't be returned under warranty. A gift that never arrives. The college kid without batteries for a week. The shipment was never about the battery; it was about the thing on the other end.
