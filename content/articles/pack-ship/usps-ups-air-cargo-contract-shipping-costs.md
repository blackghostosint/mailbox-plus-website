## The Scene That Starts Everything

You are standing at the counter with a box in your hands, watching the clerk type. The rate comes up on the screen and you blink. It is more than it was last month. You ask why, and the clerk shrugs — rates go up, that is what they do.

But there is an answer, and it is stranger than you think.

Your package is not just paying for fuel and labor. Part of what you are paying for is the cost of moving _other_ mail by air — mail that did not need to fly — because the United States Postal Service signed a contract it now has to honor.

The Postal Service's own numbers, released August 7, show the consequences. Air transportation costs between USPS facilities jumped 4.7% year over year to **$509 million** in the quarter ending June 30. The agency's explanation, buried in the earnings report, is that it is "shifting certain package volume from highway transportation back to air to meet our service standards and contract requirements."

Translation: your mail is flying because a contract says it has to.

## The UPS Air Cargo Contract

Here is the mechanism, in plain terms.

The Postal Service has a contract with UPS for air cargo. Under that contract, USPS guarantees a minimum average daily volume of mail moved by air — a floor it has to hit every day, whether the volume makes sense or not.

The problem is that Priority Mail volume is plummeting. People are not paying for expedited shipping the way they used to, so USPS is not generating enough air-worthy mail to reach the contract floor on its own. The agency's Office of Inspector General reported last month what happens next: to avoid paying penalties for missing the minimum, USPS shifts **First-Class Mail and Marketing Mail** — the ordinary letters and flats that should travel cheaply by truck — onto airplanes.

Think about that for a second. A letter that costs pennies to haul down the highway in a trailer gets loaded onto a cargo plane, burning jet fuel, because a contract demands it. The OIG said it plainly: if package volumes keep falling, USPS "will likely shift even more First-Class Mail and Marketing Mail into the air network to avoid higher costs even though that is inconsistent with the longer-term goal of reducing reliance on high-cost air transportation."

So the Postal Service is doing the expensive thing on purpose — to satisfy a contract it signed with a competitor-turned-partner.

## What It Actually Feels Like

For you, this is not a line item on a federal balance sheet. It is the number on the screen at the counter.

It is the Ground Advantage label that costs a little more than it did last spring. It is the Priority Mail quote that makes you double-check the weight. It is the moment in October, right before the holidays, when you price out shipping ten gifts and decide to hand-carry three of them instead.

The frustration is that it feels random. Rates drift up and nobody tells you the real reason. You wonder if you are being nickel-and-dimed by a bureaucracy, and the truth is worse: the cost structure itself is bent out of shape by an obligation that has nothing to do with getting your package where it needs to go.

And it is not just you. The Postal Service raised prices an extra **8% in April** on package services like USPS Ground Advantage and Priority Mail — a temporary hike scheduled to last until January 17, 2027 — to offset exactly these cost pressures. Revenue from package shipping went up 7.7% even though the number of packages _dropped_ 3.4%. That is the whole story in two numbers: fewer packages, more money, because the agency has to close a hole.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

A postal system exists for a simple reason: to move things from here to there, reliably and affordably. Every decision the agency makes should serve that purpose.

Moving letters by jet so a contract's volume floor is met serves no postal purpose at all. It is the opposite of a postal purpose. It is a financial obligation wearing a mailbag.

Here is the principle: when an institution's internal contracts start dictating how your mail travels — and therefore what you pay — the system has stopped serving the customer. The customer became a revenue source for someone else's agreement. You should not be paying jet-fuel premiums for mail that should be on a truck.
