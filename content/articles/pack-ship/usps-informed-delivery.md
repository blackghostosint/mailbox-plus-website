## You Already Know the Check Is Coming. So Why Does the Mailbox Still Surprise You?

The check from the buyer of your old truck was supposed to land Tuesday. It's Friday. You've checked the mailbox three times today and the carrier hasn't even turned onto your street — and you had no way of knowing which until you walked out to the curb.

The Postal Service already solved the not-knowing part. Letter-sized mail that moves through their automated sorting equipment gets photographed, and a free service called Informed Delivery emails you those photos before the mail reaches your door. Most people have never turned it on.

Here's what it does, how to sign up, and — just as important — what it won't fix on its own.

## The Blind Wait

The problem is not the Postal Service's speed. Here's the mechanism: the sorting equipment already photographs letter-sized mail as it moves through the network — the picture exists long before the carrier reaches your street. But that data stays locked inside a system you can't query. The default is zero visibility. The letter is either in the mailbox or it isn't. You're asked to make plans around information that already exists but isn't yours.

That information gap has real costs:

- **You rearrange your day around a maybe.** You stay home for a signature delivery that, it turns out, isn't on the truck. Or you leave, and the driver leaves the slip.
- **You can't tell a delay from a loss.** When the check that was supposed to arrive Tuesday hasn't come by Friday, you don't know whether it's slow, misdelivered, or gone.
- **You find out about problems after they happen.** The first sign that a package was taken off your porch is the empty spot where it should have been. You're reacting, not watching.

None of this is your fault. The mail network was built to move paper, not to talk to you. Informed Delivery is USPS's attempt to close that gap — and it does a real chunk of the job for free.

## What You Actually Get

Once your account is active, one email lands each morning: the Daily Digest. It shows grayscale images of the address side of your letter-sized mail — the envelopes that were scanned in the sorting network on their way to you. Some larger items, like catalogs and magazines, show up in color. Under the mail images, you get status updates on your incoming and outbound USPS packages.

At 7:30 in the morning, the digest lands in your inbox: a grayscale photo of the county envelope, a scan event for the package you shipped your sister, maybe a note that the carrier finished your street. You're holding a coffee, not standing at the curb. You know what's coming before you hear the truck.

You can also turn on extra notifications: text or email alerts for package status, and a Mail Delivery Notification that tells you when the carrier has actually finished delivering to your address that day.

## Who Can Sign Up

Informed Delivery is free to customers. Eligibility works like this:

1. You need a residential, business, or PO Box address in an eligible ZIP Code. Most ZIP Codes are.
2. Your mailbox has to be uniquely coded. Some apartment buildings and condos aren't — the system can't tell which unit an image belongs to. The sign-up flow at informeddelivery.usps.com checks your address and tells you straight.
3. Enrolling with a business street address or a PO Box requires a USPS.com business account.

USPS verifies your identity and address during enrollment, which is the point — a service that photographs people's mail before it arrives would be a disaster if anyone could enroll for anyone's address. Set it up, answer the verification step, and your account is yours.

## What It Won't Do

**It's USPS-only.** Informed Delivery shows USPS mail and USPS packages — it doesn't provide FedEx or UPS tracking, so their boxes don't show up in the digest. If your online orders mostly arrive in brown trucks or purple-and-orange ones, the morning email tells you about a fraction of what's coming to your door.

**A photo is not a lock.** You can watch the envelope with the card in it arrive on your screen at 8:40 in the morning. The mail still sits in an unattended mailbox for the rest of the day. The image tells you what's coming; it doesn't guard it.

**Some addresses just can't enroll.** Shared or non-unique mailboxes are excluded, and if your address isn't eligible yet, USPS asks you to check back later. There's no workaround inside the service.

**Tracking is visibility, not custody.** You can schedule a redelivery or leave delivery instructions for your carrier, and those help. But the package is still headed to a porch or a lobby where nobody has to be home.

## How It Works — Setup in Three Steps

1. **Create or sign in to a USPS.com account** at informeddelivery.usps.com and enter your home address. The site checks eligibility on the spot.
2. **Verify your identity.** USPS confirms the address is yours before the service turns on. This step exists to protect you, not to slow you down.
3. **Turn on the notifications you want.** In your settings, keep the Daily Digest on, add package alerts by text or email, and opt in to Mail Delivery Notifications if you want the "it's here" note each day.

That's the whole setup. No hardware, no subscription, no fee.
