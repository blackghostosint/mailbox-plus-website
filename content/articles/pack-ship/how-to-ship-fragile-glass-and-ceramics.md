You did everything right. You wrapped the vase in bubble wrap until you could barely see the glaze. You wrote FRAGILE on three sides in black marker. Here is what nobody told you: the carrier's liability for damage depends on how the package was packed, and the standard is written in their own terms — the shipper is responsible for packing the contents adequately and securely. A fragile sticker is a request, not a guarantee. What actually protects the piece is structure: cushioning measured in inches on every side, a box inside a box, and air space that absorbs the shock a single box transmits straight to the glass.

Here is the exact method UPS and FedEx publish in their own packaging guidelines but never hand you at the counter — and how we build that box at our shop in Concord Township so you never make that phone call.

## The Villain: The One-Box Wrap

The system working against you is not a careless driver. It is a packaging method that looks identical to correct packing but fails the physics of a sort facility — and it is completely legal, extremely common, and fully the shipper's responsibility.

It works like this. You take the item, wrap it in a layer or two of bubble wrap, and place it in a box with a handful of peanuts. The wrap is real. The effort is real. But three failure points are built in:

- **The bubble wrap is a single layer, and the item still touches something rigid.** Bubble wrap cushions compression — it does almost nothing against a hard sideways shove unless there are multiple wraps and layers, especially on corners and edges, which is exactly how UPS instructs it be used.
- **There is no second box.** The outer carton is the only barrier between the shelf edge, the conveyor roller, and the falling box above it. Fragile goods are precisely the case where UPS recommends the two-box method, with the outer box larger than the inner box all the way around.
- **The cushioning migrates.** Loose peanuts settle and shift in transit. UPS calls for a minimum of about two inches of loose-fill cushioning around lightweight items — and without a rigid inner box holding its position, the item works its way toward the wall of the carton over hundreds of miles of vibration.

Then the villain closes the trap. The package arrives broken, you file a claim, and the claim is reviewed against the packaging itself. Carriers publish detailed guidelines — box strength, cushioning thickness, wrapping items individually, sealing seams — and their terms state plainly that it is the shipper's responsibility to ensure the contents are properly packed. A package that does not meet the published standard is not a carrier failure. It is a packing failure, and the claim denial says so in one line.

You are not stupid for wrapping it the way most people wrap it. You were never told the standard. Nobody prints the standard on the label.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Nobody hands you the standard before you need it. That is the whole injustice.

The carriers publish excellent packaging guidelines — genuinely. UPS explains the two-box method step by step. FedEx publishes a full general packaging guide with cushioning specs. USPS tells you to shake the closed box and add cushioning if you hear anything shift. The information is free and public.

But you meet it after the loss, not before it. The checkout page never asks, "is your item fragile, and did you double-box it with three inches of cushioning in and around the inner carton?" The label form does not stop you. The drop-off clerk who accepted the package without comment was not certifying your packing — acceptance is not an indication that the package meets the carrier's standards; the carriers say that in their own terms.

So a system whose rules are public and reasonable behaves, at the moment of loss, like a trap: the standard exists, you did not know it, and the consequence lands entirely on you. A two-dollar piece of foam — three inches of it in and around an inner box, per the carriers' own spec — is the entire difference between a delivered keepsake and a denied claim.
