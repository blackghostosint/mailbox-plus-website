## The Errand That Should Be Simple

You have a box that needs to go out today. It should take one trip to one place, and you should leave knowing exactly where your package is and who has it.

Instead, you open the FedEx site, type in your ZIP code, and the list of drop-off locations looks different from last time. A station you used is no longer listed. The hours on one listing don't match another. You are left guessing which address is real before you have loaded anything into the car.

Here's the thing most people shipping from Lake County don't know: the list is changing on purpose, right now, in the middle of the busiest shipping season of the year. This article explains what FedEx is actually doing, why the map keeps moving under your feet, and how to ship from one address that is not going anywhere.

## Say It Plainly: The Vanishing Station

FedEx calls it Network 2.0. FedEx built its company on two separate shipping networks — Ground for everyday packages and Express for overnight and time-sensitive ones. For decades those networks ran their own stations, their own trucks, their own routes. That duplication costs money, so FedEx is merging them.

On its earnings call this month, FedEx confirmed the overhaul is finished in Dallas, San Francisco, Phoenix and Washington D.C. The carrier is entering the holiday season with 65% of its eligible volume flowing through roughly 630 stations optimized for the combined network — up from 45% and nearly 490 stations at the end of June. So far it has shuttered more than 260 stations, with an overall goal of over 475 closures. The project wraps up in 2027 and is expected to save the company a cumulative $2 billion.

Where does the $2 billion come from? Overlapping driver routes and unnecessary facilities. Read that again from your side of the counter: the plan literally works by removing drop-off points. FedEx pauses the work during peak season every year, and after the holidays it heads to Los Angeles, Oklahoma City and Charlotte. Which markets follow those has not been announced. When a wave does reach Northeast Ohio, some locations near you will close or change what they accept, and the announcement will arrive as a finished fact — not a heads-up.

## How It Feels, Honestly

It's the moment you're standing in your driveway with a prepaid label and a box, phone out, re-reading a location listing that sent you to an address where nothing ships anymore. You're not confused because you did something wrong. You're confused because the infrastructure changed and nobody told you.

Or it's the seller version: you've built a routine around one drop-off spot — you know the parking, you know the counter, you know what time the last pickup happens — until the day the routine is gone. You rebuild it, and quietly you start hedging every shipment, budgeting extra time in case the map moves again. That low-grade uncertainty is the real cost. It follows you into every shipping week of the year.

## It Doesn't Have to Work Like This

FedEx can merge its stations. But its balance sheet shouldn't decide where you spend your Tuesday. When your drop-off location is a line item in someone else's efficiency plan, its address is never really yours to count on.

You should be able to hand a package to a person, at a counter, at an address you don't have to look up. You should walk out knowing the package is logged, staffed, and moving — regardless of which facility chain the carrier's network routes it through. A counter, a person, a receipt. That used to be normal.
