## You Know How This Saturday Goes

You have the box. The label is printed. The tape is on. All that is left is the drop-off — a two-minute errand.

But it is Saturday, and you work 9-to-5 all week, so Saturday is the only day you have. The post office on Erie Street closes its retail counter at noon on Saturday. The line starts forming by 9:30. By the time you park, walk in, and count the people ahead of you, you are doing the math on whether you should have just mailed it Monday and driven to work late.

It should be simple. You hand over a box. Someone scans it. You get a receipt. Ninety seconds, done, and the rest of your day is yours.

That is exactly how it works at Mailbox Plus in Concord Township. One counter takes UPS, FedEx, and USPS drop-offs — all of them — and on Saturday we are open until 2 PM. Here is how to get your Saturday back.

## The Saturday Squeeze

The mechanism squeezing your weekend is not laziness or bad luck. It is the structure of Saturday hours at single-carrier locations.

The Willoughby post office at 4040 Erie Street lists Saturday retail hours of 9:00 AM to noon on the postal service's own location finder — a three-hour window, and the only one all week that works for anyone with a weekday job. Everyone in Willoughby, Willoughby Hills, and downtown who works 9-to-5 lands in that same block. The line is longest precisely when you are free to stand in it. (Hours are set by the postal service and do shift; check the current posted hours before you go.)

Single-carrier retail stores have the same structural problem from the other side. The UPS Store on Som Center Road handles UPS — and only UPS. If your label is USPS or FedEx, that store cannot accept it, so the errand is not done; it has just moved. FedEx drop boxes solve the hours problem but create a new one: no receipt, no scan confirmation, and no answer if the label peels or the box is odd-sized.

So your one errand becomes a scheduling puzzle. USPS packages go to the post office. UPS returns go to the UPS store. FedEx returns go to a staffed FedEx point or a drop box you have to trust. Three errands, three parking lots, three lines — or one Saturday morning gone.

## What It Actually Feels Like

You are holding the box against your hip. The line has not moved in five minutes. A man ahead of you is shipping six packages, each one being weighed, measured, and priced individually. You shift your weight. You check the time on your phone. You have a birthday party at 1:00 and a hardware store run between here and there.

The clock on the wall says 11:40. The sign says the counter closes at noon — and it closes at noon, not a minute later, because the window has a lock. You are now doing risk math about a cardboard box.

This is a 90-second transaction consuming a Saturday morning. That gap — between how little the errand requires and how much it takes — is where the frustration lives. You are not annoyed about shipping. You are annoyed about what the wait costs you: the lazy breakfast, the kid's game, the three unhurried hours you planned.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

Dropping off a package is not a complicated service. It is a scan and a receipt. A two-second transaction dressed up as an hour-long ordeal by hours that ignore working people and counters sized for a slower era.

You should be able to hand any labeled package — any carrier's label — to any staffed counter within a short drive of your house, any day the store is open, and leave with a scan confirmation before your coffee gets cold. The technology for that has existed for decades. The only thing missing is a counter that accepts all of them.

That is not a fantasy. It is a business decision. Some stores made it. Some did not.
