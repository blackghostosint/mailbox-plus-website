## Your Postage Price Is About to Get a Weekly Refresh

If you sell online, you already budget shipping to the penny. You know what a Ground Advantage label costs on a two-pound box to your most common zones, and you build that number into your prices. Starting in January 2027, USPS plans to make that number move — every single week. The Postal Service announced on October 26, 2026 that it will add a fuel surcharge to its package services, recalculated weekly from government-published diesel and jet fuel prices. It should be simple: one label, one price, known when you buy it. A price that drifts with the fuel market is none of those things. 

## The Weekly Shuffle

For years, USPS charged no fuel surcharge on these products — that is the part that changes in January 2027. FedEx and UPS publish fuel surcharges that update weekly against the same Energy Information Administration price indexes — if you have ever wondered why two ground labels cost different totals in the same week, that table is part of the answer. We covered that mechanism already in [our fuel-surcharge explainer](/articles/fuel-surcharge-ratchet/): most of what you pay above the base rate was set by the carriers, not by diesel.

Now USPS is joining them. According to the Fuel Surcharge Implementation FAQ the agency posted on PostalPro, the new surcharge applies to domestic Ground Advantage, Parcel Select, Priority Mail, and Priority Mail Express — including the matching return services — for retail and commercial shipments alike. Ground Advantage and Parcel Select prices follow diesel. Priority Mail and Priority Mail Express follow jet fuel. The surcharge is a percentage of base postage only, and the percentage can rise or fall every week.

Look closely at the fine print, because that is where the shuffle lives. USPS says it will publish each new percentage just three to five days before it takes effect on diesel-based services, and three to four days ahead on jet-fuel services. There is no announced starting percentage, no formula, and no stated minimum or maximum. And per the FAQ, a package mailed with a label bought at last week's percentage is subject to postage due.

There is a wrinkle worth knowing: two days after USPS published the FAQ, it pulled it from PostalPro. No explanation was given, and it is not clear whether the removal signals any change to the plan. So treat everything here as the plan as announced in late October 2026 — not as settled fact. That is precisely the problem with a weekly price: even the plan itself moves.

## What Lands on Your Desk

Here is what this looks like from your side of the screen. You list an item at $24 with free shipping, priced off last month's label. A buyer orders. You print the label — and the fuel percentage underneath it changed four days ago, silently. Maybe it is a hair cheaper. Maybe it is not. Either way, the margin you calculated is now a guess you already sold.

Sellers shipping through marketplaces have spent 2026 getting hammered by exactly this: negotiated Ground Advantage discounts that appeared without warning in June, targeted increases in July, September changes that hit certain rates with a single day's notice, then October's geographic pricing groups and holiday increases stacked on top. One shipping-software company publicly stopped sending routine rate-change alerts because carriers now change prices multiple times a week. The weekly fuel fee does not just add a cost — it adds a cost that re-arms itself every seven days, on top of a schedule that is already hard to track.

And the pressure behind it is not easing. USPS raised package revenue 7.7% year over year in fiscal Q3 2026 even while package volume fell 3.4% — rates, not demand, are doing the work. The agency posted a $2.5 billion net loss that same quarter. When the Postal Service called its temporary 8% price increase "a necessary bridge to a permanent mechanism" back in March, this weekly fee is the bridge landing on the other side.

## Why a Price You Cannot Predict Is Not a Price

You quoted one number on Tuesday. On Friday, the label prints a different one — higher. That is not a price; that is a moving target you already sold.

The old fuel-surcharge model at least had a rhythm you could learn: rates changed on schedule, the tables were published, and the bigger driver of increases turned out to be the carriers themselves. The weekly model removes even the rhythm. When USPS framed its 8% increase as less than one-third of what competitors charge for fuel alone, it was making a fairness argument — and maybe a fair one. But the mechanism it chose to close that gap is one where you find out the new number a few days before you pay it, with no ceiling published and no floor.

You did not do anything wrong here. You did not misprice your items or misread the rate chart. The system changed under the price, which is exactly the kind of thing a counter is for.

## What Guessing Costs You

Run the math on a guess. If your shipping cushion is off by even a small amount per order, and you ship a few hundred packages through a season, the shortfall comes straight out of the same margin you use to restock inventory. And the failure mode is not just overpaying — it is postage due on a package mailed at last week's percentage, which means an interrupted delivery and an annoyed customer, over a fee you never saw.

There is a quieter cost too. It is 9:40 p.m. and you are back on the rate page, coffee cold, comparing this week's fuel table to last week's — while the orders you could be sourcing and listing sit in a pile. The carriers changed the price; they do not get your evening too.
