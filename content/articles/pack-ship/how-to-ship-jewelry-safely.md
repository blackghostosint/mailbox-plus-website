## You Sold the Ring. Now the Scary Part Starts.

The listing is live, the payment cleared, and the piece that has been sitting in your drawer is finally someone else's. Now you have to put it in a box and hand it to a system that loses things, and you already know the two ways this goes wrong: the package arrives broken, or it arrives fine and the buyer claims it never did — and either way, the insurance you thought you bought quietly does not apply.

You paid for shipping and coverage, expecting safety. Instead, you got a system built so the denial is legal.

The fix is knowing the three rules the platform help pages never put in one place: pack to the carriers' published standard, buy coverage that matches what the piece is actually worth, and hand the box to a person who scans it. Here is how each one works.

## The Part Nobody Warns You About

The system working against you is not a thief on a porch. It is a coverage gap that is completely legal, printed deep in carrier terms, and invisible until you need the money.

Here is how it operates. When you buy shipping through an online platform or a carrier's retail label flow, you often get a default level of coverage included with the label. It sounds like insurance. What it actually is, for jewelry, is a policy with a low ceiling and a packing condition attached:

- **FedEx caps declared value on jewelry at $1,000 per package.** Its own jewelry guide states it plainly: jewelry shipments are "normally limited" to a $1,000 maximum declared value, and its Declared Value Advantage program (an application-only option for recurring shippers) is what lifts the ceiling. UPS's tariff caps jewelry shipments at $1,000 per package on international moves, with higher limits only to eligible destinations under special arrangements. The $2,500 gold bracelet you shipped on a standard retail label was under-covered the second the label printed — the standard checkout had no box to raise that ceiling, and the programs that do (FedEx's application-only tier, UPS's special arrangements) require setup before you ship, not a click at checkout.
- **USPS handles it differently — the coverage is real but the security tier matters.** USPS explicitly recommends Registered Mail for valuable items like jewelry; Registered Mail is the postal service's highest-security tier, with insured value available up to $50,000. Ordinary labels with standard insurance leave a valuable parcel moving through the network with the same handling as a paperback.
- **The packing condition comes standard in every carrier's terms.** All three carriers state in their liability terms that the shipper is responsible for packing contents adequately. When a claim opens, the reviewer asks for the packaging — and a bracelet in a single layer of bubble wrap inside a padded envelope fails their published standard before handling is ever questioned.

Then the platform's flow closes the trap. Marketplace labels route your sale onto whatever service the buyer selected. The default coverage tiers were never designed for jewelry, the ceiling is not shown next to the buy button, and the denial email arrives weeks later quoting terms you were never asked to read.

You are not careless for missing it. The checkout flow is designed so you never see it.

## The Five-Minute Packing Standard

If you are packing it yourself, this is the standard, in order:

1. **Wrap and box-in-box.** Each piece individually wrapped in padding. Small rigid inner box, pieces separated so they cannot touch. Outer carton with roughly 2 inches of cushioning on every side. Shake the sealed box — if anything shifts, add cushioning; USPS's own packing guide says exactly that. One hard line from FedEx: jewelry valued over $500 cannot go in a padded envelope — it needs a sturdy corrugated box. This is the method the carriers' published guidelines describe, and it is what a claim reviewer looks for.
2. **Cover it before the label prints.** The ceiling is the thing to check while you can still change the answer: FedEx $1,000 per package on jewelry, UPS $1,000 on international jewelry moves, USPS Registered Mail up to $50,000. A piece worth more than the ceiling needs third-party insurance or a different tier — decided at the counter, with the sale price known, not in a dispute email six weeks later.
3. **Get a signature for anything that matters.** Signature confirmation means "delivered" means handed to a person — not photographed on a mat. On eBay it is not even optional at the top end: orders of $750 or more require signature confirmation for the seller to be protected in an item-not-received case. It is the single cheapest defense against the not-received claim.
4. **Hand it to a staffed counter and keep the receipt.** The scan starts the tracking record with a person present. The dated receipt is your evidence of custody.
5. **Photograph the packed piece before sealing.** A photo of the item inside its cushioning — with the piece visible — is the evidence that ends most "it arrived damaged / it was never in the box" disputes in minutes. Take thirty seconds to take it.

## What Nobody Tells You You're Paying

**The money.** An under-covered or unpacked-to-standard claim is a denial letter, not a settlement — and the denial arrives after the refund, so you have paid the buyer and the carrier. **The confidence to sell the next one.** This is the quiet cost. The drawer fills back up with pieces you never list because the last shipment cost you a week of sleep and $400. Every unsold piece is a listing fee you never charged yourself but paid anyway.
