## The Scene That Starts Everything

It is a weeknight in September. You are half-watching TV, phone in one hand, when a woman in a blue polo steps out of a glowing portal in the middle of someone's workday. A plumber needs something printed and shredded before the day ends. She handles it. The screen says The UPS Store.

You have now officially met Blu — the first brand character The UPS Store has run in its entire history, launched September 1 in a national, multiyear campaign spanning TV, digital, social, and print. The spots pair her with NFL player and small business owner Aidan Hutchinson, running on Paramount streaming and CBS through early November. In one ad she helps a plumber with printing and shredding. In another, a custom sneaker brand needs a business mailbox.

If you live in Concord Township, here is the part the commercial does not mention: down the road from the franchised store in that campaign, on Fredle Drive, there is a store that offers the same menu — mailboxes, printing, shredding, notary — plus something no character in a portal can offer. We will get to that.

## The One-Word File

First, credit where it is due, because the problem Blu exists to solve is real. Every brand on earth gets filed in your memory under one word. Walmart is "cheap." Geico is "cheap-ish, funny lizard." The UPS Store got filed under "shipping." That is the file the campaign is fighting — and it is an expensive file to reopen.

So The UPS Store built a character to walk America through the rest of the menu: printing, shredding, mailboxes. The trade press calls it unifying franchisee messaging — thousands of independently owned locations, one shared story instead of every store re-explaining itself. The company even has a name for the mechanism: Blu appears through a portal, a repeatable device built to run as endless short "help me with this" vignettes. It is smart work, honestly constructed, and it follows a wave of the same playbook — Stanley introduced a wisecracking toolbox named Stan in August, Liberty Mutual rolled out Liberty Biberty in July, Crocs hatched a mascot named Niles. When your product is routine, a character is how you buy your way back into people's attention.

And notice the timing. The campaign lands while parent company UPS is chasing what its CEO calls "premium volume" — small and medium businesses, health care, B2B — after cutting Amazon deliveries by more than half and shedding tens of thousands of jobs. The ads are not decoration. They are the visible edge of a company-wide pivot toward exactly the customer carrying a shoebox of orders into a shipping store.

The campaign is not the problem. The campaign is the confession. The UPS Store is spending a national, multiyear budget to teach customers something true: a shipping store is more than shipping. Which happens to be a thing an independent store down the road has been telling Lake County all year — for free, in plain English, without a portal.

## What It Actually Feels Like

Here is what matters about the ads, and it is not the comedy. The scenarios are real. The plumber who needs a printout and a shred bin today is a real customer. The custom sneaker seller who needs a business address that is not his apartment is a real customer. We see both of them every week at our counter, because that moment — "I need this handled before Friday" — is exactly when a small business owner goes looking for a counter instead of a website.

The ads get the moment right. Where they go quiet is the moment after. You remembered the brand, you drove to the store, the box is on the counter — and now what? The character does not ride along. What happens next depends entirely on what is behind the counter, and that is the one thing no national campaign can change from the outside.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

There is a difference between being memorable and being better, and the whole multiyear campaign lives on one side of that line.

A character can fix a memory problem. Give it a portal and a polo and a season of NFL advertising, and people will genuinely stop filing The UPS Store under "shipping." Fine. But a character cannot fix a structural fact: every one of those thousands of franchised locations exists to sell UPS. The exclusivity is the franchise model, not a staffing choice. So when your box is on the counter, the only price you will hear is the UPS price. Not because anyone is hiding anything — because the store structurally cannot show you FedEx, USPS, or DHL next to it. If UPS is the cheapest carrier for your package, great. If it is not, the most memorable store in America is still a one-price store.

That is the gap between the ad and the counter. Being reminded a store exists is not the same as getting a fair deal at it. Memorability is a marketing outcome; it is not a shipping outcome.

## How It Works

Blu has a portal. We have a door. Three steps:

1. **Walk in with your item** — package, print job, shred box, or the mail question you have been putting off.
2. **Put it on the counter.** We weigh it, quote all four carriers on the screen turned toward you, and answer the questions the website could not.
3. **Pick your price and leave.** Most counter visits run a couple of minutes, with tracking and a receipt before you are back in the car.

## What You Lose by Not Acting

Habit is a tax and it is collected quietly.

**Money.** When one carrier is your only carrier, you pay the one price on the shelf. Across a year of shipping — holiday season, customer returns, the occasional fragile thing — the spread between the cheapest carrier and the only carrier is real money leaving a small business.

**Time.** The plumber in the ad needs it handled today. So do you. A store that can print, shred, take the return, and ship the package in one stop — with the price settled on the spot — is the difference between one errand and three.

**The default itself.** A national campaign is designed to move you off autopilot toward a brand. Fair enough. But once you are off autopilot, staying on it — any autopilot — is how overpaying becomes a routine you never notice.
