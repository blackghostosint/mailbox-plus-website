## The Drop-Off Is Not the Delivery

Same-day delivery means the package reaches its recipient today; a same-day drop-off only means you hand it over today. The difference decides what you can honestly promise.

The label is straight. Tape grips the box seam; the counter receipt slides toward you. You stop with the question you hoped the label had answered: “Will this arrive today, or just leave today?” You do not want to explain a delay because the service name was unclear.

At Mailbox Plus, you can compare available carrier options at the pack-and-ship counter and see what each service says. Here’s how to separate today’s handoff from today’s delivery before you pay.

## The Promise Changes Hands at the Counter

The label peels at the corner; as tape pulls across cardboard, you hear the dry rasp. The receipt comes across the counter, and for a moment the errand feels done. Then “ships today” appears in the confirmation, and the question returns: does the carrier promise arrival, or only dispatch?

They prove something narrower: the shipping transaction has started. Delivery time is a separate promise. It depends on the service you bought and its rules for the origin, destination, package, and handoff. If the wording is vague, ask the seller or carrier to say plainly whether it promises dispatch today or arrival today.

That’s the hidden trap in “ships today.” A business may mean it will prepare or hand over an order today. That can be useful, but it is not the same as telling you the item will arrive today. A fast-looking checkout badge can blur those two moments until the parcel is already on its way.

## What a Same-Day Service Actually Commits To

A real same-day service is a specific option, not a mood or a shortcut. The carrier has to accept that shipment for that destination under that service’s conditions. The date you need, the origin and destination, the package, and the way it enters the carrier’s network all matter.

FedEx says its SameDay services deliver time-sensitive shipments within hours, depending on flight availability. It also says those shipments cannot be dropped off at FedEx retail locations or drop boxes. A person who needs that service must arrange it through the carrier’s stated process rather than assume a regular storefront handoff will qualify.[1]

UPS describes Express Critical as a same-day service for urgent, time-sensitive, emergency, or high-value goods. UPS says most shipments arrive in 2-12 hours, while noting that transit times vary with the specific shipment and urgency. That is a specialized option, not a blanket promise attached to every UPS label.[2]

USPS describes Priority Mail Express differently: its page lists 1-3 day delivery by 6 p.m. to most U.S. addresses, with exceptions. It says the scheduled date depends on origin, destination, and drop-off time. That can be a fast option, but the published range is not a same-day arrival commitment.[3]

## The Carrier Details That Change the Answer

The carrier logo alone does not answer whether your parcel can reach its recipient today. You need the service name and the destination-specific commitment. Packages dropped off at the same counter can have different options because their routes, sizes, weights, and acceptance conditions differ.

Start with the actual delivery address, not just the city name. Ask whether the service reaches that destination today and what time the carrier must receive the shipment. A package given to a store after its pickup may not move on the same schedule as a package accepted through a special pickup arrangement. “I dropped it off today” is a sender’s timestamp; the service commitment belongs to the carrier. If you’re sending USPS to Alaska, Hawaii, or a U.S. territory, our [guide to noncontiguous delivery times](/articles/usps-noncontiguous-delivery-times/) covers another reason the destination matters.

Then ask how the carrier must take custody. FedEx’s SameDay page says the service cannot be tendered at its retail drop-off locations and requires the shipment to be created through its SameDay process. That’s an easy detail to miss if you search for a quick counter instead of reading the service conditions.[1]

Ask what the quoted delivery time includes. Is the carrier promising the delivery date, or giving an estimate? Does the commitment depend on flight availability, destination coverage, package characteristics, or a particular pickup method? If the answer is “it should get there,” you still do not have a clear commitment.

Finally, ask what happens if the shipment misses the stated date. A money-back guarantee, when the service offers one, is governed by its terms and exceptions.[3] Keep the receipt and the service name, and read the carrier’s conditions before you rely on a refund as a backup plan.

## Why the Package Feels Late Before It Moves

You can do your part and still lose time at the handoff. Maybe the order is packed, the address is correct, and you reached a counter before closing. If the selected service does not match the deadline, or the carrier can’t accept it through that route, the package can be “shipped” without being on a same-day delivery path.

You watch the box leave and rehearse the update you might have to send: “I thought it would arrive today.” You don’t want to sound careless or make excuses when the phrase at checkout never told you whether “today” meant dispatch or delivery. That is not a failure to plan; the promise was never clear enough to plan around.

The safer question is not “Can I get this out today?” It is “What service can deliver this package to this address today, and how does the carrier need to receive it?” That question separates speed at the counter from speed across the route.

## The Cost of Choosing a Deadline Blind

When the deadline matters, guessing can cost more than the postage. You may pay for a premium service that never qualified for the route, miss the handoff method the carrier requires, or tell a customer a date that the service never promised. Then you spend the next day explaining a delay that no amount of refreshing can undo.

If the recipient can wait, don’t pay extra for urgency you don’t need. If the date is fixed, don’t buy a label whose only promise is today’s handoff. Match the service commitment to the deadline, then compare the price.

When you compare options before paying, you can choose with your eyes open. If none of the available services can meet the date, you can tell the recipient now instead of discovering the gap after the parcel has disappeared into the network.
