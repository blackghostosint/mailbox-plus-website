## Why the Bank Says No

If you have been turned away at a bank branch off Lake Shore Boulevard or Vine Street, it was not the teller being lazy. It was the system working exactly as designed.

Inside a retail bank branch, the notary commission almost never belongs to the person at the teller line. It belongs to an assistant manager who is mid-way through a home equity application, or a personal banker who gets measured on loans opened this quarter. A notarization pays the branch nothing — Ohio law caps the fee for an in-person notarial act at five dollars. Five dollars against a loan officer's hourly revenue target is a rounding error, so the branch builds friction: notary by appointment only, "our notary is out until Thursday," or the quiet internal rule that certain documents — real estate packets, trusts, anything with legal exposure — are simply declined.

There you are: a homeowner with a legitimate deadline, standing in a lobby holding a document that needs ninety seconds of ink, being told to come back when a salaried employee's calendar permits. You did nothing wrong. You just brought a five-dollar errand to an institution that is built to sell six-figure products.

## What Ohio Law Actually Requires

Strip away the appointment portals and the lobby vetting, and a notarization is a narrow, well-defined act.

Here is all Ohio law actually asks of you. Show your photo ID. Let us watch you sign — or take your formal acknowledgment. Let us press the seal. Ninety seconds, not a loan interview. The state even caps the fee for an in-person notarial act at up to five dollars, because it is meant to be a routine errand anyone can finish on a lunch break.

The same statute draws hard lines that protect you: a notary may not notarize a document that is incomplete or blank, and may not execute a jurat without administering the oath or affirmation in person. Those rules are the reason we ask you to bring your document fully filled out but unsigned — signing in advance is the single most common mistake that forces a do-over.

That is the entire transaction. No cross-sell, no account review, no "is there anything else I can help you with today." Any commissioned notary can do it in about ninety seconds. The only question is whether the notary is actually on duty when you show up.
