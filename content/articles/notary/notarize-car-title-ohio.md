## Selling a Car Shouldn't End in a Rejected Title

You found a buyer. You agreed on a price. The car is theirs the moment you hand over the keys — except on paper, it isn't. In Ohio, the sale isn't finished until the title moves, and the title can't move until the seller's signature on the back of it is notarized. Skip that step, and the buyer walks into the title office and walks out with your car still legally yours.

Ohio's requirement is one line long: the seller completes the assignment of ownership on the back of the title and signs it in front of a notary. Then the buyer takes that signed, notarized title to a Clerk of Courts title office and finishes the transfer. One stamp stands between a handshake and a done deal — and it's the step that trips up more private sales than any other.

## The Assignment Line Nobody Reads Until It's Too Late

Flip the title over. There's a block on the back called the assignment of ownership, and it's where private sales live or die. The state requires the seller to fill it out completely and have the signature notarized. Not "get around to it." Not "the buyer can handle it later." The seller signs, a notary watches, a seal goes on.

The notary is the state's fraud check. Anyone can forge a name on a line. A notary's job is to verify that the person signing is actually the person the title names, watching them do it in real time. So the law puts the notarization on the seller's signature — the one signature that proves the owner really is giving the car up.

And there's a trap in the wording people miss: the notarization applies to private sales. Since an April 2023 change in the Ohio Revised Code, when a licensed dealer is a party to the transfer, no notarization is needed on the title paperwork at all. That's the dealer exception. It does not apply when you sell your neighbor your Camry. If you heard "Ohio stopped requiring title notarization," that's the dealer rule getting garbled — on a casual sale between private parties, the seller's signature still has to be notarized.

## What It Actually Feels Like When You Get It Wrong

It feels like a wasted trip that costs someone real money — and worse, like standing in front of your buyer as the amateur. You've got the title in hand, that thin sheet of cardstock the state won't let you laminate or copy, and your pen has already been across the signature line once. The buyer sits in a title office chair with a folder full of paperwork, and the clerk slides the title back across the counter: the assignment is signed but not notarized, or one of the owners listed on the front never signed at all. Everyone named on that title has to complete the assignment — both spouses, the co-owner, everybody. You sold the car in good faith. The paper says otherwise, and until that seal bites into the cardstock, the liability rides with your name, not the person driving it.

Or it happens earlier, on the phone: the seller spends an afternoon calling banks that only notarize for their own customers, libraries whose notary is off today, offices that want an appointment days out — while the buyer waits, and the transfer clock keeps running. Title transfers have to happen within 30 days of the date of sale, or a late fee gets assessed. The person who usually eats that fee is the buyer, and the buyer is standing in your driveway holding cash.

## Why a One-Line Signature Shouldn't Take All Day

Notarizing a signature is a short, simple act. The notary checks a government-issued ID — current, or expired no more than 3 years — watches you sign, stamps the document, done. That's the whole act. Ohio law even caps what a notary may charge: up to 5 dollars for a standard notarial act.

So the friction isn't the law. It's availability. Notaries work at banks, libraries, and offices where the stamp is a side duty assigned to whichever employee happens to hold a commission. When that person is at lunch, the stamp is at lunch. A requirement this small shouldn't force a seller to circle the county for a weekday afternoon, and a buyer shouldn't inherit a late fee because a stamp was hard to find.

And the near-misses? We catch them at the counter, because most people mess this up four ways:

- **Don't sign before you arrive.** The whole point is that the notary watches you sign. A pre-signed assignment has to be redone on corrected paperwork, and an altered line on a title creates exactly the kind of doubt a title office exists to reject.
- **Bring every owner listed on the front.** All owners must complete the assignment and sign before the notary.
- **Bring your ID.** A driver's license, state ID, or passport — current or expired less than 3 years.
- **Leave the blanks that stay blank.** Fill in what the assignment asks the seller to fill in; don't improvise in fields meant for the title office.

## How It Works

You walk in with the title and your ID — unsigned, with everyone named on the title present. No forms to prepare, no online scheduler. The notary verifies your identity against the name on the front, watches you sign the assignment, and presses the seal into the paper while you watch.

Then you hand the title to your buyer, and they take it to their county Clerk of Courts title office, pay the title fee — $18 statewide, up to $23 in some counties — plus sales tax on the purchase price, and the transfer is done. Remind them of the 30-day window.

Buying rather than selling? The same counter works for you: get the seller to come in and notarize before money changes hands, so you're not the one standing in a title office with a defective assignment. (If the Ohio title is held electronically, the seller uses the state's casual-sale form instead of the back of a paper title — and that form's seller page gets notarized the same way.)

Out-of-state title? You'll also need an $8.00 out-of-state VIN inspection — available through a deputy registrar license agency, a participating Clerk of Courts title office, or a licensed Ohio dealership — before the vehicle can be titled in Ohio. The assignment itself can be notarized at our counter first.

## What a Skipped Notarization Actually Costs

- **A rejected transfer.** The title office sends the buyer away, and the car stays in your name — which means liability questions stay in your name too, on a car you don't have anymore.

- **The do-over chain.** A crossed-out line or a missing owner's signature can mean tracking down corrected paperwork after the car is already gone — the single most annoying version of this errand.
