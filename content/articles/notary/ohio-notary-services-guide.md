You have a document that needs to be notarized. Maybe it is a car title transfer. Maybe it is a power of attorney for an aging parent. Maybe it is a legal affidavit, a real estate closing document, or a permission letter for a minor child traveling abroad.

Whatever it is, you need a notary public to watch you sign it and apply their official stamp. It should be simple. It is one signature and one seal.

But if you have tried to get a document notarized in Lake County recently, you already know it is anything but simple. The bank notary is "at lunch." The library notary "left at 2:00." The title company charges forty dollars and requires an appointment three days out.

You drive to three different places. You spend an afternoon chasing a stamp. And when you finally find someone, they ask for your ID, stamp the paper, and charge you a fee that varies wildly depending on where you ended up.

It should not be this hard to get a signature verified. But in Ohio, the notary system is fragmented, understaffed, and confusing — especially if you do not know how it actually works.

## The Notary Availability Gap

Here is the problem: notaries in Ohio are individuals who passed a state exam and registered with the Secretary of State. They work at banks, libraries, insurance agencies, title companies, and shipping stores. But most of these places do not employ notaries as their primary function.

The bank teller who is also a notary might be on the loan desk today. The library clerk who is also a notary only works Tuesdays and Thursdays. The insurance agent who is also a notary is in a meeting until 3:30.

Notary services are treated as an afterthought — a secondary duty assigned to an existing employee who happens to hold a commission. When that employee is sick, on vacation, or busy with their primary job, the notary stamp is unavailable.

This creates the Availability Gap: a notary exists on paper but is not accessible in practice. You call ahead and are told, "She is here, but she is in a meeting until 4:00." Or you walk in and are told, "Our notary left early today."

The system is nominally functional but practically broken. And it wastes hours of your time.

## What Notarization Actually Is

Before we go further, let us clarify what notarization is and is not in Ohio.

A notary public is a state-authorized witness whose job is to:

1. Verify your identity by checking a valid government-issued ID
2. Confirm that you are signing the document voluntarily and under your own free will
3. Witness your signature in person
4. Apply their official notary seal and record the notarization in their journal

That is all. The notary does not read your document for legal accuracy. They do not advise you on whether you should sign it. They do not verify the content of what you are signing. They simply verify that the person signing is who they claim to be.

In Ohio, the maximum fee a notary can charge is $5.00 per signature under state law (ORC 147.07). Some notaries charge less. Some charge the full $5.00. Some — like mobile notaries who travel to you — charge additional travel fees.

The notarization itself is straightforward. The system around it is not.

## Why It Should Not Be This Way

In Ohio, the state sets the maximum fee at $5.00 per signature for a reason — notarization is supposed to be an accessible, affordable public service. It is a basic function of document verification that every citizen needs at some point.

But the state does not ensure that notaries are available. It simply licenses individuals and leaves availability to their employers. The result is a patchwork of part-time notaries, variable fees, and unpredictable hours.

A notary service that is open when you need it, staffed consistently, and charges the state maximum fee should not be a rare find. It should be the standard.

## How to Prepare for Your Notary Visit

To make your notary visit as fast as possible, bring these three things:

1. **Your unsigned document.** Do not sign it before you arrive. The notary must watch you sign. If you sign it at home, the notary cannot notarize it — you will need a new copy.
2. **A valid government-issued photo ID.** A driver's license, state ID card, passport, or military ID all work. The ID must be current — expired IDs cannot be accepted.
3. **Payment.** $5.00 per signature in Ohio. Cash, card, or check accepted at our counter.

If you are notarizing a car title in Ohio, the title must be signed in front of the notary by both the seller and the buyer (unless one party is a dealership). Bring the title and the IDs of all signers.

If you are notarizing a power of attorney, make sure the document is complete — blank sections cannot be notarized. The notary must see the final, filled-in document.
