## The Notary Who Answers to the Branch Manager

If your notarization keeps slipping to "next week," it is not bad luck. It is how retail banks are built.

Inside a bank branch, the notary commission belongs to someone else's job description. The person holding the seal is usually an assistant manager opening home equity lines or a platform officer buried in loan files. Notarizing your document is an unpaid courtesy that pulls them off revenue work, so branches protect themselves: notaries "only by appointment," internal rules that quietly exclude real estate packets and living trusts, tellers who check a calendar before they check the clock.

That is the mechanism working against you. Ohio sets the notary's fee at a maximum of five dollars per in-person notarial act — so a bank will never make money on your stamp. The branch is happy to spend five minutes of its staff on a mortgage pitch, and structurally unwilling to spend one on your signature. You are not being difficult. You brought a ministerial act to a sales floor.

## What a Stalled Signature Is Really Costing You

Put off the stamp and the cost compounds faster than the errand is worth:

- **Deadlines do not move.** Purchase agreements, deed transfers, court filings, and school or travel consent forms all carry dates that do not care that your bank's notary works Tuesdays. A signature delayed a day can delay a closing, void a filing window, or strand a traveler.
- **Your credibility eats the delay.** When you tell an attorney, a title officer, or the relative counting on that power of attorney "I still haven't gotten it notarized," you are the one who looks unprepared — for something you could have finished on a lunch break.
- **Improvised signatures create redo work.** The kitchen-table instinct — sign it now, get the stamp later — produces a defective document. A notary cannot legally stamp a document already signed outside their presence for a jurat, so you reprint the packet and start over, days deeper into the same deadline.
