## What Should Take Two Minutes

Somebody overseas just asked you for an "apostilled" document. A birth certificate, a power of attorney, a diploma, a background check — the details vary, the moment doesn't: you're holding paperwork you thought was finished, and a person on another continent is waiting on it.

And now you're being told it needs two stamps. A notary. Then an apostille. Which comes first? Who issues the second one? Why can't the same person do both?

Here's the plain answer: **a notary and an apostille are two different certifications from two different offices, and they are done in order — notarize first, apostille second.** The notarization you can get done at our counter in Concord Township, walk-in, no appointment — [here's how a walk-in notary visit works](/articles/where-to-get-documents-notarized-walk-in/). The apostille is issued only by the Ohio Secretary of State in Columbus, and no notary anywhere can issue it for you. This article walks through exactly what each stamp does, what it costs, and the order to do them in so your packet doesn't come back rejected.

## Meet The Rubber Stamp Confusion

The reason this trips people up is that the two stamps look similar — ink, seal, official-looking certificate — and apostille resellers and courier services have every incentive to keep them blurred together.

Here's the mechanism. A notary public is a state-commissioned individual. Their job is to verify **you**: that the person signing the document is who they claim to be, that they signed willingly, in the notary's presence. That's it. The notary never evaluates the document's content.

An apostille is something else entirely. It's a certificate issued by the Ohio Secretary of State that verifies the **notary's** signature and commission — a certification about the certification. It exists for one reason: your document is going to a country that belongs to the 1961 Hague Convention, and that country has no way to check an Ohio notary's commission on its own. The apostille is the state vouching for the stamp (Ohio Secretary of State, "Understanding Apostilles and Authentications in Ohio," ohiosos.gov).

Because it's confusing, a whole tier of paid intermediaries exists to make it stay confusing. Search "apostille service Ohio" and you'll find flat-fee offers that bundle a notarization, a filing, and courier runs into a single flat price on top of the $5-per-document state fee.

And one small trap catches almost everyone: if your document needs a correction or an extra page, you cannot just staple something on after the fact. The state's own FAQ warns that removing, detaching, or reattaching an apostille certificate invalidates the certification. You start over.

## Simple Things, Made Complicated on Purpose

It shouldn't take a service bureau to handle a two-step process the state publishes in plain English. The rules aren't hidden:

- The Ohio Secretary of State decides whether your destination country needs an apostille (Hague Convention member) or an authentication (not a member) based on where the document is going (ohiosos.gov FAQ).
- Only documents signed by an Ohio official or **notarized by an Ohio notary** can be apostilled by Ohio's office (ohiosos.gov).
- The fee is $5 per document, and mailed requests are processed in two to three days (Form 8003, last revised 07/2025).
- Translated documents get translated first, _then_ notarized, then submitted.
- FBI background checks and federal documents don't go through Ohio at all — the FBI and the U.S. Department of State handle their own authentications.

The state even publishes the checklist on the request form itself: completed Form 8003, exact fee, no cash in the mail, a self-addressed stamped envelope or prepaid express label, and the document. That's the entire process.

Say it plainly: the apostille does not validate what your document says. It only certifies that the signature on it is real. And the two are not interchangeable or competitive; one is a prerequisite for the other.

## The Cost of Leaving It Alone

Stall on this and the costs stack in three currencies.

**Time.** Every bounced or mis-assembled packet costs a full round trip of international mail. A two-day state turnaround becomes three weeks because a step was skipped.

**Money.** The state's fee is $5. The intermediaries charge a hundred to several hundred dollars for assembling what the request form explains on its own second page. And a redone notarization — because you signed at home first — is another fee and another trip.

**Credibility.** The document sitting in a foreign bureaucracy is usually attached to something real: a marriage registration, a work permit, a property closing, an adoption. The delay isn't abstract to the person waiting for it.
