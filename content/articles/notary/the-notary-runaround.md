## The Out-to-Lunch Notary

Call the mechanism what it is: the Out-to-Lunch Notary. It is not an accident, and it is not personal bad luck. It is the natural result of how large retail institutions treat notarial work.

To a commercial bank branch or a national retail chain, a notarial act is an uncompensated distraction. In Ohio, standard in-person notarial fees are capped by state statute (under Ohio Revised Code 147.08, a notary public may charge up to five dollars per standard in-person notarial act). To a branch manager whose monthly bonuses depend on originating three-hundred-thousand-dollar home loans, opening commercial credit lines, and selling wealth-management portfolios, a five-dollar state-regulated transaction is an operational nuisance.

Banks don't hire dedicated notaries. Instead, they ask a junior teller or a customer service representative to take the state exam and keep a seal locked in their desk drawer. But that employee has a primary job: processing deposits, running loan underwriting, or covering customer accounts. When that person goes to lunch, takes a sick day, attends an internal compliance seminar, or simply gets tied up in a forty-five-minute loan interview in a back office, the branch's notary service instantly evaporates.

The sign in the window remains, but the service is gone. You stand in front of an apologetic teller who gives you the standard corporate shrug and suggests you try their branch two towns over. They aren't trying to be difficult. They are simply operating inside a business model where your urgent signature doesn't generate enough profit to justify keeping someone behind the desk.
